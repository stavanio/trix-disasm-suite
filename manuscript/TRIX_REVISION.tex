\begin{abstract}

Runtime safety governors are often evaluated as though their effectiveness were
independent of how the upstream policy was trained. We show that this assumption
can fail. We introduce TRiX (Transparent Real-time eXplainable Control), a
neuro-symbolic execution governor that separates action proposal from command
authority by accepting, projecting, rejecting, or declaring infeasible robot
commands under explicit task-conditioned admissibility constraints.

Using a rebuilt six-task DISASM-Bench, we evaluate two regimes separately:
post-hoc governance of frozen policies trained without the restriction, and
filter-aware learning in which the restriction is active during training. The
central empirical result is that the value of a governor can change
substantially between these regimes. Holding the runtime restriction fixed, PCB \texttt{box\_clip} yields
0.0\% safe completion when applied post hoc to nominally trained SAC policies
and 100.0\% when SAC is trained with that same restriction active. By
contrast, on CRANK, policy adaptation does not eliminate the observed advantage
of the implemented task-conditioned governor: the static restriction remains
at 0.0\% safe completion while the task-conditioned governor reaches 49.4\%. On screw
extraction, matched analytical projection, matched QP, and TRiX produce equivalent
task-level outcomes when supplied the same admissible-set specification,
showing that the principal distinction is not projection machinery alone.

We further evaluate six procedurally generated admissible-set families on
200 held-out instances to separate representation adequacy from individual
task semantics. A controlled foundation-model study evaluates actual VLM
outputs from three provider runs on 91 shared PCB images, yielding 273
recorded decisions. Thirty-five images elicit an inadmissible proposal in at
least one run, with no image common to all three failure sets. Across 257
grounded commands replayed with and without governance, TRiX prevents all
31 observed constraint-violation cases and introduces none during the
40-step evaluation horizon. Together, these results show that execution-governor performance depends
on policy adaptation, retained action authority, task context, and constraint
representation rather than on a universal ordering of safety methods. TRiX is
therefore positioned as an explicit execution-authority boundary whose value
depends on the proposal and physical constraint it mediates.

Finally, on a Seeed reBot B601-RS, an excessive Joint~2 proposal of
\(50^\circ\) was projected to \(18^\circ\), with only the projected target
physically executed, while inadmissible requests were rejected before physical
target generation. Three video-documented trials of the frozen final
implementation additionally completed a grasp-and-return sequence with the
object retained.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Electronic waste presents an unusually difficult manipulation domain because disassembly combines uncertain initial conditions with physical failure modes that may be irreversible. Devices arrive with variation in fasteners, adhesives, component wear, deformation, corrosion, and assembly history; batteries, printed circuit boards, connectors, and threaded interfaces additionally impose physical constraints that cannot be inferred from task semantics alone \cite{ref1,ref2,ref3,ref4,ref5,ref6}. A robot may therefore select the correct component and the correct semantic operation while still proposing a physically inadmissible execution.

Recent progress in Vision-Language Models (VLMs), Large Language Models (LLMs), and learned manipulation policies makes this distinction increasingly important \cite{ref8,ref9,ref10,ref11,ref13}. These models can reason about objects, task order, and manipulation goals, but their outputs ultimately enter a physical control system. The central question addressed in this work is therefore not whether a foundation model can produce a plausible robotic action, but under what conditions that proposal should be granted execution authority.

We study this question in robotic disassembly, where task-conditioned physical constraints provide a useful testbed for runtime governance. Examples include coupling translation and rotation during threaded extraction, conditioning allowable forces on manipulation phase, expressing constraints in task-relative coordinate frames, maintaining planarity during component extraction, and respecting preventive limits around fragile or hazardous components. These constraints differ not only in numerical threshold but also in the state information and admissible-set representation required to enforce them.

TRiX addresses this interface by separating \emph{action generation} from
\emph{action authority}. A learned policy or foundation model proposes an
action; TRiX evaluates that proposal against an explicit task-conditioned
admissibility model before physical execution. The revised study focuses on a
consequence of this separation that is often hidden in safety-layer
comparisons: the behavior of a governor cannot in general be evaluated
independently of the policy that proposes actions to it. A restriction imposed
after training acts on a proposal distribution that never adapted to the
available command authority; the same restriction present during training can
reshape that distribution. We therefore ask not whether structured projection
always wins, but when post-hoc governance requires richer authority, when
policy adaptation makes a simpler restriction sufficient, and when missing
task context or representation remains limiting even after adaptation.


\subsection{The Promise and Limits of Foundation Model Planners}

VLMs and LLMs have substantially expanded the semantic capabilities available to robotic systems \cite{ref8,ref9,ref10}. A model can identify components, infer relations among them, and propose manipulation primitives without requiring a manually enumerated program for every product variant. For disassembly, this creates a plausible path toward handling heterogeneous devices whose component layouts and valid operation sequences vary substantially across instances.

Semantic competence, however, does not imply physical admissibility. A model may identify the intended operation correctly while proposing a command that is inconsistent with the current manipulation phase, task coordinate frame, coupled kinematics, or allowable contact condition. Conversely, a model may refuse to propose an action, produce a semantically incorrect command, or refer to content that is not grounded in the observed scene. These outcomes should not be conflated.

Accordingly, throughout this work we reserve \emph{hallucination} for outputs that invent or refer to nonexistent content, and distinguish it from refusal, semantic error, ungroundable output, and physically inadmissible action. Our controlled VLM experiment uses 91 shared images from 28 base configurations and a common command interface. We compare archived provider runs descriptively, retaining repeated visual conditions within each configuration rather than treating them as independent physical trials.

\subsection{From Generic Safety to Task-Conditioned Admissibility}

Several established approaches address constrained robot learning and runtime safety, including constrained reinforcement learning, action shielding, projection methods, recovery policies, model-predictive control, and control barrier functions. These methods differ substantially in their assumptions and guarantees, and we do not claim that any of these classes is generally incapable of representing nonlinear, state-dependent, or time-varying constraints.

Our narrower concern is the representation and task information actually available at the execution boundary. If a filter is restricted to independent per-axis limits while the task requires a coupled condition, that representation may admit commands outside the intended admissible set or discard legitimate task authority. If the constraint changes with manipulation phase but the filter does not receive phase information, even an otherwise adequate set representation can enforce the wrong action set. Similarly, a task-frame constraint cannot generally be reproduced by a fixed world-frame restriction unless the relevant orientation or transformation is represented.

These distinctions are particularly important because post-hoc governance and
filter-aware learning are different experimental regimes. When a policy is
trained without a runtime restriction, the governor must operate on proposals
generated without knowledge of the authority it will preserve or remove. When
the same restriction is present during training, the policy can reorganize its
proposal distribution around that authority. A poor post-hoc result therefore
does not imply that the restriction is intrinsically unusable, and a successful
filter-aware result does not imply that the same restriction can safely govern
an independently trained policy.

This regime distinction is a central empirical question in the revised study.
We test when policy adaptation removes an apparent need for richer governance
and when representation or task context remains material even after adaptation.


\subsection{The Linearity Gap}

We retain the term \emph{Linearity Gap} from the submitted formulation, but use it more precisely as one instance of a broader representation gap: a mismatch between the admissible actions required by a task and the representation available to the runtime governor.

Screw extraction provides the simplest analytical example. For a screw with pitch $p$, admissible axial and rotational velocity commands satisfy

\[
v_z = \frac{p}{2\pi}\omega_z .
\]

Independent bounds on $v_z$ and $\omega_z$ do not encode this coupling. The important point is not that linear or classical methods are inherently incapable of expressing screw safety; rather, a governor that represents these command dimensions independently cannot reproduce the required relationship without additional coupled structure.

The revised experiments show that this issue is not uniform across tasks. On some tasks, a correctly specified simple restriction is sufficient. On others, the relevant limitation is missing phase information or an inappropriate coordinate frame rather than nonlinearity alone. Moreover, policies trained with a restriction active can sometimes adapt to restrictions that perform poorly when imposed on an independently trained policy. The practical question is therefore not whether structured projection always wins, but which representation and context are required at the point where execution authority is exercised.

We evaluate this question both in the six disassembly tasks and in a separate procedural benchmark containing box, ellipsoidal, polytope, state-scaled, rotating, and phase-gated admissible-set families. The procedural benchmark provides the general test of representation adequacy; individual disassembly tasks are interpreted according to the specific context, specification, and set structure they exercise.


\subsection{Our Contribution: TRiX}

We introduce TRiX, a neuro-symbolic execution governor for learned and generative robotic policies. TRiX places an explicit boundary between action proposal and physical execution: an upstream model proposes a command, and TRiX evaluates that command against task-conditioned admissibility constraints before actuation.

TRiX is designed around three properties:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Explicit admissibility.} Constraints are represented in a form appropriate to the physical task, including coupled, state-dependent, phase-conditioned, and task-frame conditions where required.

\item \textbf{Minimal intervention.} When a proposal is inadmissible but a feasible correction exists, the governor seeks a nearby admissible command rather than replacing the upstream planner.

\item \textbf{Auditable execution authority.} Acceptance, projection, rejection, and infeasibility are explicit runtime outcomes whose physical basis can be inspected independently of the learned policy.
\end{itemize}

We make four principal contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A separation between post-hoc governance and filter-aware learning.}
We show that execution-governor performance depends on whether the proposal
policy was trained with the restriction active. The same simple PCB
restriction that fails when imposed on a frozen policy reaches 100.0\% safe
completion after filter-aware training, while CRANK shows that adaptation does
not universally eliminate an advantage for the implemented task-conditioned
governor.

\item \textbf{A neuro-symbolic execution-authority architecture.}
We formulate robotic action governance as task-conditioned admissibility
between learned proposal generation and physical execution, with explicit
ALLOW, PROJECT, REJECT, and INFEASIBLE outcomes and analytical results scoped
to command admissibility rather than universal closed-loop stability.

\item \textbf{A systematic analysis of representation adequacy.}
We rebuild DISASM-Bench with frozen evaluation protocols and supplement the
task-level experiments with six procedurally generated admissible-set families
and 200 held-out instances, separating policy adaptation, task context, set
representation, and solution mechanism.

\item \textbf{Foundation-model and physical execution evaluation.}
We evaluate actual VLM outputs from three provider runs on 91 shared images
from 28 base configurations. The 273 decisions yield different inadmissible
image sets across runs. Paired executions of the 257 grounded commands show
31 constraint-violation cases without governance and zero with TRiX over the
40-step evaluation horizon.

On the B601-RS, physical intervention trials demonstrate
\texttt{ALLOW}, \texttt{PROJECT}, and \texttt{REJECT} behavior before
actuation, and three video-backed trials of the frozen final implementation
complete a grasp-and-return sequence with the object retained.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these results support a different claim from the submitted
manuscript. The relevant unit of analysis is not the governor in isolation but
the interaction among the proposal policy, the action authority retained by
the governor, and the physical admissibility model. Structured governance is
most valuable when a simpler restriction removes task-essential authority or
lacks required task context; when sufficient authority remains and the policy
can adapt, a simpler restriction may be enough.


\section{Related Work}

TRiX lies at the intersection of constrained reinforcement learning, runtime action filtering, control-theoretic safety, foundation-model robotics, and robotic disassembly. These areas address different parts of the safety problem: learning policies that respect constraints, modifying actions before execution, establishing forward-invariance conditions, generating semantically useful robot actions, and reasoning about domain-specific manipulation hazards. Our goal is not to argue that one of these paradigms is universally insufficient, but to position task-conditioned execution governance relative to them.


\subsection{Constrained Reinforcement Learning}

The Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) framework optimizes expected return subject to expected constraint costs \cite{ref17}. Representative approaches include CPO \cite{ref16}, PPO-Lagrangian \cite{ref18}, RCPO \cite{ref19}, and related adaptive Lagrangian methods \cite{ref20}. These methods incorporate safety objectives into policy optimization and are particularly relevant when the policy itself is expected to adapt its behavior in response to constraint feedback.

Their objective differs from the primary deployment setting studied here. TRiX is concerned with \emph{post-hoc execution governance}: an upstream policy or foundation model may have been trained independently of the physical constraint representation, and a runtime mechanism must decide whether the resulting proposal should be executed. Constrained RL can reduce the frequency with which an adapted policy proposes undesirable actions, but this does not by itself define the admissible command set imposed on an independently trained or externally supplied policy.

For this reason, the revised experiments separate nominal policies governed after training from policies trained with a restriction active. This distinction avoids interpreting policy adaptation and runtime enforcement as the same experimental question.


\subsection{Projection-Based Safety Layers and Recovery Policies}

Runtime intervention methods modify or replace actions before execution. Safe exploration methods such as SafeLayer \cite{ref21} learn local constraint information and use it to correct proposed actions, while Recovery RL \cite{ref22} learns a recovery mechanism that activates near unsafe regions. These approaches establish the important architectural principle that task optimization and safety intervention can be separated.

TRiX adopts the same high-level separation but focuses on a different question: whether the information and admissible-set representation available to the intervention mechanism are sufficient for the physical task. A componentwise restriction may be entirely adequate for a separable constraint, while the same representation may be unsuitable for a coupled command relationship. Likewise, a filter with an adequate set representation can still be incorrect if it lacks the task phase or coordinate transformation on which the constraint depends.

We therefore avoid treating ``linear safety layers'' as a single empirical baseline class. In the revised benchmark, simple restrictions are named and evaluated according to the information and set form they actually implement. Learned constraint representations are analyzed separately from trained policy baselines.


\subsection{Control Barrier Functions}

Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) provide a control-theoretic mechanism for rendering a safe set forward invariant \cite{ref23,ref24}. For a safe set

\[
\mathcal{C}=\{x:h(x)\geq 0\},
\]

a common formulation imposes a condition of the form

\[
\dot h(x,u) \geq -\alpha(h(x)),
\]

and selects a control input satisfying this condition, often through a quadratic program. Barrier-function methods have also been combined with learned controllers and uncertain dynamics \cite{ref25}. Related learning with control Lyapunov functions addresses stability under model uncertainty \cite{ref26}.

Importantly, the CBF framework is not limited to linear safe sets or axis-aligned constraints. Nonlinear state constraints can be represented directly through nonlinear barrier functions, and the broader CBF literature includes extensions for more complex relative-degree, time-varying, and task-dependent settings. We therefore withdraw the submitted manuscript's categorical claim that CBFs cannot represent the nonlinear or state-dependent constraints considered by TRiX.

The relationship between CBF filtering and TRiX is instead empirical and architectural. On the SCREW task, we implement a matched QP using the same analytical admissibility specification supplied to TRiX. The two mechanisms produce equivalent task-level safety performance when given the same constraint. The implemented comparator solves a quadratic program over the analytical command set; the experiment tests solution mechanism under that common specification. Barrier-function synthesis and state-space forward invariance require separate conditions.

TRiX differs principally in how the execution contract is organized. The symbolic/task layer selects the applicable admissibility model, including task phase and coordinate context where required; the runtime layer then returns an explicit ALLOW, PROJECT, REJECT, or INFEASIBLE outcome. For tasks admitting a closed-form projection, this can avoid an online optimization solve, but computational form is a deployment property rather than a claim of greater representational power over CBFs.

\subsection{Foundation Models for Robot Planning}

Foundation and vision-language models have expanded the range of semantic information that can be used for robotic decision making. PaLM-E grounds language in embodied multimodal observations \cite{ref8}; RT-2 maps visual-language inputs to robot actions \cite{ref9}; SayCan combines language-model planning with learned affordance estimates \cite{ref10}; and Code-as-Policies generates executable robot programs from language \cite{ref11}. Embodied-agent work such as Voyager \cite{ref12} and robotics-oriented analyses of large language models \cite{ref13} further illustrate the growing role of generative models in action selection and task reasoning.

These systems address semantic grounding, planning, affordance selection, or action generation. Physical admissibility can additionally depend on quantities and constraints that are not captured by semantic correctness alone: the current manipulation phase, coupled motion, task-relative orientation, contact limits, or latent physical state. We therefore treat the foundation model as an \emph{action proposer}, not as the sole authority on whether a proposed command should reach the plant.

This distinction motivates the controlled VLM study in Section~5. Rather than synthetically injecting a predefined ``hallucination'' class, we expose the three provider runs to the same images and distinguish refusal, grounding failure, command inadmissibility, constraint-violation cases, and destructive completion. Semantic correctness is not separately annotated in the archived results.


\subsection{Robotic Disassembly}

Robotic disassembly has been studied in the context of recycling, battery processing, manipulation planning, and human--robot interaction \cite{ref6}. The domain is challenging because component condition, fastening mechanisms, contact geometry, and material hazards vary substantially across products and over their service lives.

Our scope is narrower than the full disassembly problem. We do not attempt to solve perception, task sequencing, grasp synthesis, or general contact planning. Instead, DISASM-Bench uses disassembly primitives as controlled settings in which to study the physical admissibility of proposed actions. This enables the evaluation to distinguish task progress from constraint satisfaction and to examine cases involving coupled kinematics, phase-dependent constraints, preventive force limits, and sequential prerequisites.


\subsection{Positioning of TRiX}

Table~\ref{tab:submitted-1} summarizes these distinctions. TRiX should therefore be understood as an execution-governance architecture rather than as a replacement for constrained RL, recovery methods, or CBFs. These approaches can be complementary.

Constrained RL can adapt the proposal policy itself. Recovery and shielding methods can intervene at runtime. CBF-based controllers can provide formal invariance mechanisms when an appropriate barrier model is available. TRiX contributes an explicit neuro-symbolic boundary in which task state and symbolic context select a physical admissibility model before a learned or generative proposal is granted execution authority.

The revised evaluation consequently separates three questions that were conflated in the submitted manuscript:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Specification:} does the method enforce the same physical constraint and margin?
\item \textbf{Representation and context:} can the available admissible-set model express the task requirement using the information supplied to it?
\item \textbf{Solution mechanism:} given the same admissible set, how is a corrected control obtained?
\end{enumerate}

The matched QP experiment addresses the third question; the task-level and procedural-geometry studies address the second; and the BATTERY matched-specification control demonstrates why the first must be checked independently. This decomposition provides the basis for the comparisons used throughout the revised manuscript.


\section{Theoretical Framework}

TRiX is an execution governor: an upstream policy proposes an action, while a
task-conditioned admissibility model determines whether that proposal may be
executed directly, must be modified, or must be rejected. This section defines
that execution boundary and gives four limited propositions describing when
simple restrictions are equivalent to exact projection, when missing task
context necessarily induces conservatism, and when probabilistic tightening
removes usable control authority. These results replace the broader
input-to-state stability claim in the submitted manuscript.


\subsection{System Model and Execution Boundary}

Let $x\in\mathcal{X}$ denote the observed physical state and let
$c\in\mathcal{C}$ denote task context, such as the active primitive,
manipulation phase, or task-frame orientation. An upstream policy or planner
produces a proposed command

\[
\tilde u = \pi(x), \qquad \tilde u \in \mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}.
\]

For each $(x,c)$, TRiX associates an admissible action set

\[
\mathcal{A}(x,c)\subseteq\mathcal{U}.
\]

Membership in $\mathcal{A}(x,c)$ means that the command satisfies the
\emph{modeled command-level admissibility conditions} for the current task
state and context. It does not, by itself, establish universal plant safety:
unmodeled dynamics, tracking error, perception error, omitted hazards, or an
incorrect physical specification can still invalidate the intended safety
interpretation.

The governor implements four explicit outcomes:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{ALLOW:} if $\tilde u\in\mathcal{A}(x,c)$, execute the proposal;
\item \textbf{PROJECT:} if $\tilde u\notin\mathcal{A}(x,c)$ and a feasible
      correction exists, return a nearby admissible command;
\item \textbf{REJECT:} if the proposal violates a non-projectable symbolic or
      task-state rule, do not forward the proposal for execution;
\item \textbf{INFEASIBLE:} if the specified admissible set provides no usable
      command authority for the required operation, surface that condition
      rather than silently relaxing the specification.
\end{enumerate}

In the following Euclidean analyses, command coordinates are normalized by
the declared task scales. For a nonempty closed convex admissible set, the
unique projection is

\[
\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde u)
=
\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal{A}(x,c)}
\frac{1}{2}\lVert u-\tilde u\rVert_2^2 .
\]

Equality-defined manifolds are therefore one important special case of the
more general admissible-set formulation rather than the universal form of
every TRiX constraint.


\subsection{Canonical Example: Helical Coupling}

The ideal zero-tolerance screw relation couples axial and angular velocity as
$v_z=k\omega_z$, where $k=p/(2\pi)$ for thread pitch $p$. The projection
metric must also be specified: adding squared errors in m\,s$^{-1}$ and
rad\,s$^{-1}$ without their command scales does not reproduce the implemented
objective.

Let $S_v$ and $S_\omega$ be the axial and angular command scales, and define
\[
a=\begin{bmatrix}v_z/S_v\\ \omega_z/S_\omega\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \alpha=\frac{kS_\omega}{S_v}.
\]
The ideal helical set in these normalized coordinates is
$\mathcal A_0=\{a:a_v=\alpha a_\omega\}$. Its Euclidean projection is
\[
\Pi_{\mathcal A_0}(\tilde a)
=\frac{\alpha\tilde a_v+\tilde a_\omega}{1+\alpha^2}
\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Physical commands are recovered by multiplying the two components by
$S_v$ and $S_\omega$. This line is an analytical illustration. The implemented
SCREW v3 experiment instead uses a finite helical tolerance, command bounds,
and a radial-force bound; its normalized admissible polygon is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:submitted-2} and specified in Appendix~B.1. The analytical
relationship is not independent experimental validation of the physical model.


\subsection{Representative Inequality and Context-Conditioned Sets}

Other tasks require inequality or context-conditioned admissible sets rather
than equality manifolds.

For PCB extraction, the revised action interface contains two tilt-torque
components and a lift component. A representative command-level admissible set
has the form

\[
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{PCB}}
=
\left\{
(\tau_x,\tau_y,F_z):
\sqrt{\tau_x^2+\tau_y^2}\leq \tau_{\max},
\;
|F_z|\leq F_{\max}
\right\},
\]

with the operational projector using the benchmark's declared robust interior
margin. The tilt constraint is therefore coupled: independent clipping of
$\tau_x$ and $\tau_y$ describes a box, not the disk above.

For a phase-dependent task, admissibility is indexed by the active phase
$q$,

\[
\mathcal{A}(x,c)=\mathcal{A}_{q}(x),
\]

and for a task-frame constraint it may depend on an orientation or frame
transform $R(\theta)$. These cases motivate distinguishing the geometry of an
admissible set from the context required to select or orient that set.

For BATTERY, admissibility includes preventive force and state-dependent
physical limits. The exact thresholds and thermal dynamics are treated as part
of the benchmark specification in Section~4 rather than as a universal
geometric manifold.


\subsection{Four Limited Propositions}

The following propositions characterize representation properties of the
execution boundary. They deliberately make narrower claims than the submitted
ISS theorem.


\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Proposition 1 (Separable sets reduce to componentwise clipping).}
Let

\[
\mathcal{B}
=
\prod_{i=1}^{m}[\ell_i,r_i]
\]

be a nonempty axis-aligned product set. Then Euclidean projection onto
$\mathcal{B}$ is exactly componentwise clipping:

\[
[\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}(\tilde u)]_i
=
\min\{r_i,\max\{\ell_i,\tilde u_i\}\}.
\]

\emph{Proof.}
The squared Euclidean objective separates as

\[
\lVert u-\tilde u\rVert_2^2
=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_i-\tilde u_i)^2,
\]

and the feasible set is the Cartesian product of independent intervals.
Minimizing each scalar term independently gives the clipping expression.


\emph{Implication.}
When two methods enforce the same axis-aligned product set and the same
margin, ``structured projection'' has no representational advantage over
componentwise clipping. Any measured difference must come from another
factor, such as specification, information, optimization, or implementation.


\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Proposition 2 (A phase-blind safe restriction is necessarily
conservative when phase sets differ).}
Let $\mathcal{A}_1$ and $\mathcal{A}_2$ be two distinct admissible sets for two
task phases, and let $\mathcal{B}$ be a phase-independent restriction. If
$\mathcal{B}$ is safe in both phases, then

\[
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_1\cap\mathcal{A}_2.
\]

Consequently, when $\mathcal{A}_1\neq\mathcal{A}_2$, a phase-independent set
cannot reproduce both phase-specific admissible sets: it is strictly
conservative in at least one phase unless the difference between the sets is
irrelevant to the commands required there. Conversely, admitting an action
outside $\mathcal{A}_1\cap\mathcal{A}_2$ makes the restriction inadmissible in
at least one phase.

\emph{Proof.}
Safety in phase 1 requires
$\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_1$ and safety in phase 2 requires
$\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_2$; hence
$\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_1\cap\mathcal{A}_2$. Because the two
phase-specific sets are distinct, no single set can equal both.


\emph{Implication.}
This proposition concerns information availability, not curved geometry.
Whether the conservatism affects task completion is an empirical question:
the phase-specific authority that is removed may or may not be task-essential.


\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Proposition 3 (Worst-case conservatism of a fixed frame under
rotation).}
Consider the anisotropic rectangle

\[
\mathcal{R}_0
=
\{u\in\mathbb{R}^2:
|u_1|\leq a,\;
|u_2|\leq b\},
\qquad a\geq b>0,
\]

and its rotations
$\mathcal{R}_{\theta}=R(\theta)\mathcal{R}_0$ for
$\theta\in[0,2\pi)$. Any orientation-independent set that is admissible for
every orientation must lie in

\[
\bigcap_{\theta}\mathcal{R}_{\theta}
=
\{u:\lVert u\rVert_2\leq b\}.
\]

In particular, a symmetric world-frame box
$[-h,h]^2$ that is safe for all orientations must satisfy

\[
h\leq \frac{b}{\sqrt{2}}.
\]

\emph{Proof.}
Every vector with norm at most $b$ satisfies both coordinate limits under
every rotation, so the radius-$b$ disk is contained in the intersection.
For any vector with norm greater than $b$, choose an orientation whose narrow
axis aligns with that vector; the narrow-axis bound is then violated. Thus the
intersection is exactly the radius-$b$ disk. The corner of $[-h,h]^2$ has
norm $\sqrt{2}h$, yielding the stated condition.


\emph{Implication.}
A fixed-frame restriction that must remain valid over arbitrary orientation
can lose admissible authority solely because it lacks the relevant frame
conditioning. The CRANK study is interpreted as combining angle conditioning
and task-frame representation; it is not presented as an experimental
isolation of either factor.


\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Proposition 4 (Feasibility under probabilistic tightening).}
Consider a scalar symmetric physical limit $|y|\leq L$ with $L>0$, additive
zero-mean Gaussian uncertainty of standard deviation $\sigma>0$, and a
tightening margin

\[
m=z\sigma,
\]

where $z>0$ is the quantile implied by the declared chance-constraint risk
allocation. The tightened command interval is

\[
|y|\leq L-z\sigma.
\]

It has nonzero command authority if and only if

\[
z\sigma < L
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
z < \frac{L}{\sigma}.
\]

If $z\sigma=L$, only the zero command remains. If $z\sigma>L$, the tightened
interval is empty.

\emph{Proof.}
The radius of the tightened interval is $L-z\sigma$. Its sign directly gives
the three cases above.


\emph{Implication.}
A physically meaningful nominal constraint can become unusable after
probabilistic tightening. Such a case must be reported as specification or
feasibility failure rather than credited to the projection mechanism.


\subsection{Matched Projection and QP Equivalence}

For an affine admissibility constraint

\[
Au=b
\]

with full-row-rank $A$, Euclidean projection solves the quadratic program

\[
\min_u \frac{1}{2}\lVert u-\tilde u\rVert_2^2
\quad
\text{subject to}
\quad
Au=b.
\]

The unique solution is

\[
u^\star
=
\tilde u
-
A^\top(AA^\top)^{-1}(A\tilde u-b).
\]

Thus a closed-form projector and a correctly formulated QP are not competing
representations when they are supplied with the same affine constraint; they
solve the same optimization problem. The SCREW control applies the same
principle to a convex polytope defined by inequalities: its analytical and QP
implementations minimize the same normalized command error over the same
finite-tolerance slab and bounds. The archived policy comparison establishes
matching task-level outcomes, not a measured solver-precision or timing claim.


\subsection{Policy Adaptation Is an Empirical Finding, Not a Proposition}

The revised experiments also distinguish post-hoc governance from training
with a restriction active. In several tasks, a simple restriction that performs
poorly when placed in front of an independently trained policy becomes adequate
when the policy is trained through that restriction. This observation motivates
the Stage~1 versus Stage~2 comparison in Section~5.

We make no theorem-level claim that policy adaptation will always overcome a
representational mismatch. Whether adaptation succeeds depends on the task,
the authority retained by the restriction, the reward and learning dynamics,
and whether task-essential actions remain reachable. Policy-adaptation
dominance is therefore reported only as an empirical finding.


\subsection{What the Theory Does and Does Not Guarantee}

The propositions above concern the representation, context, and feasibility of
the command-level admissibility set. They do not imply that:

\begin{itemize}
\item the modeled constraint is physically correct or complete;
\item satisfying the command-level set guarantees safety under unmodeled
      dynamics, state-estimation error, contact transients, or tracking error;
\item exact projection can repair a misspecified threshold;
\item a reactive governor can create a missing preparatory action; or
\item TRiX is uniformly superior to CBFs, learned filters, or constrained
      policy optimization.
\end{itemize}

These boundaries motivate the model-validation, sensitivity, specification,
and sequential-constraint analyses in Sections~4 and~5.


\subsection{Proposal Inadmissibility and Physical Hallucination}

The submitted manuscript used \emph{Physical Hallucination} for any proposal
outside a modeled safe set. The revised terminology separates two concepts.

A proposal is \emph{physically inadmissible} at $(x,c)$ when

\[
\tilde u \notin \mathcal{A}(x,c).
\]

When projection is defined, the intervention magnitude

\[
d_{\mathrm{int}}
=
\left\lVert
\tilde u-\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde u)
\right\rVert_2
\]

quantifies how far the proposal lies from the modeled admissible set.

By contrast, we reserve \emph{hallucination} for a semantic or grounding error
in which the upstream model invents or refers to content not supported by the
input. A hallucinated output may be inadmissible, but the two labels are not
equivalent. Likewise, an inadmissible command can arise without hallucination,
for example from a correctly identified task executed with excessive force or
with the wrong task-frame command.

This distinction is used explicitly in the VLM evaluation in Section~5.

\section{Experimental Methodology}

The revised experiments were rebuilt around three separable questions:
(1) whether the physical constraint specification is valid,
(2) whether the runtime representation has the information and set structure
required to express that constraint, and
(3) whether different solution mechanisms behave differently when supplied
the same admissible set. This section describes the resulting benchmark,
training protocol, runtime comparators, procedural representation study,
foundation-model study, and reproducibility controls.

\subsection{Episode Outcome Contract}

A single classification function partitions every episode into exactly one of
five outcomes:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{safe completion};
\item \textbf{unsafe but intact completion};
\item \textbf{destructive completion};
\item \textbf{mechanical failure}; or
\item \textbf{timeout}.
\end{enumerate}

This contract is shared across policy algorithms and runtime-filter arms.
Task-specific violation families are additionally recorded as diagnostics.
For a thresholded physical quantity $x$ with declared limit $s>0$, normalized
exceedance is

\[
e=\max\left(0,\frac{x-s}{s}\right).
\]

The primary endpoint used for checkpoint selection and most task comparisons
is safe-completion rate. Destructive completion, normalized progress,
episode-level violation incidence, intervention magnitude, infeasibility, and
individual violation families are retained as secondary diagnostics.

\subsection{Learning Implementation}

Only two trained policy algorithms are reported: Proximal Policy Optimization
(PPO) \cite{ref31} and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) \cite{ref32}. Policies are
trained with Stable-Baselines3 v2.9.0 and Gymnasium v1.3.0 using a two-layer
MLP with hidden widths $[256,256]$. The earlier custom SAC implementation was
retired before the reported experiments, and none of its results are retained.

The Gymnasium adapter distinguishes true environment termination from
time-limit truncation. Evaluation also saves and restores NumPy random state so
that interleaved evaluation cannot alter subsequent training.

When a runtime restriction is active during training, the policy action is
interpreted as a \emph{nominal} command and the restriction is part of the
environment transition:

\[
a_{\mathrm{nominal}}
\longrightarrow
\text{governor}
\longrightarrow
a_{\mathrm{executed}}
\longrightarrow
\text{plant}.
\]

The replay or rollout buffer therefore contains the policy's nominal action,
not an assertion that the policy itself emitted the corrected command. We
refer to this explicitly as the \emph{filter-as-environment} MDP framing.


\subsection{Two Evaluation Modes}

The revision separates two questions that the submitted evaluation conflated.

\paragraph{Stage 1: frozen-policy governance.}
For each task, algorithm, and training seed, a nominal PPO or SAC policy is
trained without the evaluated runtime arm. After checkpoint selection, the
same frozen policy is evaluated through each applicable arm on the same
held-out episode seeds. Thus the arms receive proposals from the same learned
policy, and the comparison asks how well each mechanism governs a policy it did
not train.

\paragraph{Stage 2: filter-aware training.}
A second set of policies is trained with the corresponding restriction active
inside the environment transition. This asks whether the learned policy can
adapt its nominal proposal distribution to the authority retained by a
restriction. Stage~1 and Stage~2 are reported separately; one is not used as a
surrogate for the other.

\subsection{Frozen Checkpoint Selection and Held-Out Evaluation}

Checkpoint selection is fixed before the reported held-out evaluations.
For every task--algorithm--training-seed cell:

\begin{enumerate}
\item train to the predeclared task budget and save a checkpoint every
      1000 training steps;
\item evaluate every checkpoint on 30 fixed validation episodes;
\item rank contiguous three-checkpoint windows by mean safe-completion rate
      and retain the center checkpoints of the three best windows;
\item re-evaluate those candidates on 70 additional validation episodes,
      producing 100 validation episodes per candidate;
\item select lexicographically by maximum safe completion, minimum destructive
      completion, maximum normalized progress, minimum episode-level violation
      incidence, and then earliest checkpoint;
\item evaluate the selected checkpoint once on 100 held-out test episodes; and
\item repeat for ten production training seeds, $0,\ldots,9$.
\end{enumerate}

Validation and held-out test episodes are disjoint. Pilot seeds used during
pipeline development are excluded from reported results. The candidate
checkpoint set and ranking rule are part of the frozen protocol rather than
post-hoc analyst choices.

The current seed scheduler uses explicit deterministic task offsets, with a
deterministic digest fallback for future task names, rather than Python's
process-dependent \texttt{hash()} function. Historical artifacts were audited
for seed pairing; any identified mismatch is preserved in provenance and
corrected transparently rather than silently overwritten.


\subsection{Constraint Margins and Specification Controls}

For constraint families requiring stochastic tightening, the margin is derived
from a declared episode-level risk budget and episode horizon, with allocation
across applicable constraint families. Gaussian tightening is therefore
described as a chance constraint under the assumed noise model, not as a
worst-case robustness guarantee.

The implementation distinguishes three feasibility states:

\begin{itemize}
\item the tightened set is nonempty;
\item the tightened set retains positive nonzero action authority; or
\item the tightened set is infeasible.
\end{itemize}

Specification is evaluated independently of projection form. Two explicit
controls are important in the revised study. First, BATTERY includes a
matched-specification comparison in which componentwise and TRiX arms enforce
the same preventive force threshold. Second, PCB results retain the provenance
fact that the historical box and TRiX arms differed both in set form and in
effective margin; that comparison is therefore not used as an isolated proof
of geometric superiority.


\subsection{Model Validation, Sensitivity, and Solver Controls}
\label{sec:model-validation}

The analytical implementation is checked at three levels.

\paragraph{Equation and implementation checks.}
Unit and environment-level acceptance tests verify the declared state
transitions, violation families, progress metrics, constraint fingerprints,
and filter behavior. These tests establish implementation consistency with the
declared analytical model; they are not independent validation of the physical
model itself.

\paragraph{Matched solver control.}
For SCREW, analytical projection and the matched active-set QP enforce
the same finite-tolerance command polytope. An operator-splitting implementation
is also retained in the source. The archived policy comparison tests the
task-level consequence of matched command constraints. We do not report
pointwise numerical error or runtime measurements without their supporting
benchmark record.

\paragraph{Specification sensitivity.}
Twenty-one physical constants spanning the six environments are perturbed over
multipliers from $0.5$ to $2.0$, producing 105 perturbation cases. The completed
sweep checks a task-specific ordering under scripted proposals, using eight
episodes per arm and case. An ordering holds if the designated arm has fewer
destructive completions, or if both have none and that arm has at least as high
a safe-completion rate. Failure can therefore reflect lower completion without
damage. We inspect the nine failed orderings after the sweep; this is a
post-hoc diagnosis, not a preregistered test of sufficient safety conditions.


\subsection{Procedural Admissible-Set Benchmark}

To separate general representation behavior from the idiosyncrasies of the six
disassembly environments, we generate three-dimensional admissible sets from
six families:

\[
\{\text{box},\text{ ellipsoid},\text{ polytope},
  \text{ state-scaled},\text{ rotating},\text{ phase-gated}\}.
\]

The generator is frozen as specification v1.3. Separate random streams produce
120 training, 40 validation, and 200 held-out instances. The archived held-out evaluation contains 200 instances.

Each generated instance contains a required action sampled from the interior of
its exact admissible set. This action stands in for task-essential control
authority. Before evaluating the competing representations, instances are
stratified according to whether the implemented context-free axis-aligned box retains
that required action.

This stratification is deliberately structural: it is a calibration of the
criterion, not independent evidence that the criterion discovered its own
groups. The empirical comparison within and across strata evaluates how
clipping, learned affine and nonlinear models, context-free and
context-conditioned oracle approximations, and exact projection retain the
required action while remaining feasible.

The learned models are fitted separately for each evaluated geometry using
3,000 labeled state--action samples from that geometry; the nonlinear model
uses 250 fitting steps. Thus the held-out designation refers to the geometry
bank, not to transfer of a single learned model to unseen geometries. Each
method is checked on 400 sampled proposals per instance. Required-action
retention permits displacement below $0.02(\lVert u_{\rm req}\rVert+10^{-9})$.
The context-free box uses the implemented inscribed-box construction and,
for context-dependent families, the componentwise minimum across 21 sampled
contexts. We make no global maximal-volume or continuous-context certification
claim for that construction.


\subsection{Prospective Bayonet Stress Test}

A separate BAYONET experiment was preregistered to test whether the
representation criterion could make a prospective prediction rather than only
explain the original six tasks. Static and phase-gated variants were frozen
before the learned-policy runs, together with comparator arms and a competence
gate.

The primary confirmatory algorithm was SAC, with PPO treated as a secondary
replication. Policies used a 15,000-step training budget, the same frozen
checkpoint-selection rule, ten training seeds, and 100 held-out episodes.
Before observing the final comparison, the protocol declared that a variant
with no learned arm reaching 50\% completion would be classified as
\emph{uninformative} rather than as confirming or falsifying the criterion.
The outcome of this prospective test is reported in Section~5 without changing
that gate after the run.


\subsection{Controlled Foundation-Model Command Study}
\label{sec:vlm-method}

The synthetic ``hallucination injection'' experiment is replaced by a
controlled evaluation of actual vision-language-model outputs on one PCB task.
Bank specification v1.0 records the freeze date 11 August 2026 and identifier
\texttt{b3bb5dac2ef5a8fa}. It contains 91 images from 28 base configurations:
21 configurations each have normal, rotated, occluded and misleading visual
conditions, and seven additional configurations each have one partial view.
The three main strata each contain seven configurations and 28 images.

Each provider run receives the same images and prompt, with a 2,000-token
output limit. The recorded configured aliases are:
\begin{itemize}
\item Anthropic: \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-5};
\item OpenAI: \texttt{gpt-4o};
\item Google: \texttt{gemini-flash-latest}.
\end{itemize}
These strings are aliases, rather than pinned snapshot identifiers
\cite{ref36,ref37,ref38}. We use Claude Sonnet, GPT-4o and Gemini as labels
for the runs configured with these aliases. The client permits fallback within
provider pools and writes the configured alias even if a fallback succeeds.
All 273 raw outputs and parsed commands are retained; successful backend IDs,
per-call access times and retry-attempt histories are unavailable in the
recovered archive. Earlier run notes report four Gemini API timeouts that
were retried. The final Gemini file has 91 successful, uniquely identified
response records and no error rows, but the affected images and successful
variants cannot be identified from it. We neither infer that a variant switch
occurred nor assign an unrecorded snapshot to a trial. The accompanying trial
ledger retains each recorded alias and marks unavailable backend information
explicitly. The bank freeze date is not an API access date.

\paragraph{Visual tilt scaling and margin strata.}
The original bank generator applies a numerical display scale:
it stores a display angle $\phi$ and a normalized parameter
$t=\texttt{tilt\_norm}$. For the 21 configurations in the three main strata,
before rounding the saved values,
\[
b=\texttt{TAU\_ROBUST}(1-\texttt{INTERIOR}),\qquad
m=1-|t|/b,\qquad \phi=0.9t/b.
\]
Here $b\simeq0.174174$ is the normalized robust tilt-command bound and $m$
is the generator's relative margin. Replay instead restores the mechanical
curvature angle using
\[
\theta_x=tS_\tau/K_{\rm bend}=0.25t,\qquad \theta_y=0.
\]
Thus the displayed angle is approximately $20.669\theta_x$ for the main
strata. The seven partial configurations use $t=\phi/0.9$, giving
$\phi=3.6\theta_x$; these have no margin-stratum assignment. For example,
\texttt{s00\_normal.png} stores $\phi=0.3248$ rad and $t=0.0629$, whereas
replay restores $\theta_x=0.015725$ rad. Both use the recorded lift of
0.00797 m. The formulas precede rounding of the stored parameters.

The displayed tilt is a visibility-scaled cue, not a depiction of a physically
realisable intact-board state under the benchmark's bending model. In the
saved near-boundary stratum, $|\theta_x|=0.0358$--$0.042525$ rad is displayed
as $|\phi|=42.4^\circ$--$50.4^\circ$, whereas the model's fracture threshold
is $0.075$ rad ($4.30^\circ$). Interpreting those display angles as mechanical
curvature would therefore exceed the fracture threshold.

Within the main strata this positive scaling preserves tilt sign and the
ordering of tilt magnitude and generator margin; the stored values retain
that ordering. It does not preserve the numerical physical angle or make
apparent image distance to a boundary a physical safety margin. The partial
views use a different scale: the same mechanical tilt has an approximately
5.74-fold larger display angle in a main-stratum image. Margin and visual-tilt
comparisons use only the 84 main-stratum images; the seven partial views
are reported separately. Byte-identical
re-rendering from saved display parameters tests rendering reproducibility;
it cannot establish equality of displayed and mechanical tilt. The VLM bank
is separate from the six manuscript workspace illustrations.

The frozen parser maps outputs to \texttt{LIFT}, \texttt{TILT\_X},
\texttt{TILT\_Y}, \texttt{PRESS}, \texttt{HOLD}, or \texttt{ABORT}. Refusal and
ungroundable output are recorded separately. A grounded command is classified
as admissible or inadmissible by comparison with the task projector. The
archive calls the admissible category ``executable''; both admissible and
inadmissible grounded commands can be replayed. It contains no separate
human annotation of semantic correctness.

Each grounded command is held for up to 40 simulator steps in both the
unfiltered and governed arms from the same restored state and seed.
The measured endpoint is an episode-level \emph{constraint-violation case}:
at least one violation during the replay. A \emph{prevented
constraint-violation case} requires a violation in the unfiltered replay and
none in its governed counterpart. We compute this directly from the recorded
violation counts. The historical \texttt{projection\_prevented\_harm} field
combines violations with destructive completion; destructive completion is
zero in every VLM replay, so that field coincides with our violation endpoint
in these records. We do not label it a damage or fracture count. A changed
command alone receives no credit. Refusals remain in the all-image
denominator; replay outcomes use the grounded-command denominator.
This is a single-proposal study, not an autonomous VLM agent loop.

The training seed is the experimental unit for learned-policy experiments.
For each cell we report seed-level summaries across ten production seeds; the
100 held-out episodes within a seed estimate that seed's policy performance and
are not treated as independent policy replicates.

Comparisons between arms applied to the same policy use paired per-seed
differences. Table~\ref{tab:submitted-11} reports seed-paired bootstrap

and identifies the episode-seed exceptions. The submitted

For the procedural benchmark, the generated admissible-set instance is the

\subsection{Hardware Platform}

The physical platform is a Seeed reBot Arm B601-RS with RobStride
actuators and gripper, powered from a 48-V supply and commanded over
1-Mbit/s CAN. Joints~1--3 use RS-06 actuators and Joints~4--7 use RS-00
actuators. The existing MIT-style joint controller operates at approximately
200~Hz. The final grasp implementation is frozen at commit
\texttt{d296e6e} and tag
\texttt{trix-hardware-soft-grasp-success-20260828}.

The hardware execution path preserves the separation between proposal and
command authority. A manipulation action is represented as a symbolic
\texttt{Proposal}; \texttt{TrixProjector} returns an
\texttt{ALLOW}, \texttt{PROJECT}, or \texttt{REJECT} decision;
\texttt{TrixExecutionBridge} converts an approved decision to an
executor-facing command; and \texttt{TrixPhysical\-Target\-Adapter} maps the
approved command to a physical target relative to the joint configuration
captured at trial start. The implementation uses \texttt{ALLOW} when a
proposal is approved without correction.

Joint~1 is enforced as a hard physical invariant. A proposal that explicitly
requests Joint~1 motion is rejected, Joint~1 is excluded from the bridge's
supported command joints, and the physical adapter restores the captured
Joint~1 value before execution.

The final grasp sequence uses J3 model-based gravity feed-forward with
\(\alpha=0.25\). The high-level sequence is governed symbolically, while the
hardware executor contains two disclosed grasp calibrations for the specific
gripper and object: a \(+5^\circ\) Joint~2 grasp-ready positioning offset after
the deep-hover primitive, and a mapping from symbolic gripper state to the
calibrated Joint~7 open and grasp positions. These mappings implement an
approved physical primitive; they are not autonomous grasp synthesis.

For each formal confirmation trial, the evidence package preserves the console
log, passive timestamped CAN traffic, CAN state before and after execution,
source and configuration copies, process exit status, SHA256 checksums, and
continuous video. No independent force/torque sensor is used in this hardware
experiment.


\subsection{Reproducibility and Provenance}

An implementation audit identified a distinction between the later per-family
margin-policy registry and the values used by several archived executable
filters. Historical PCB, SNAP, CRANK, BATTERY, and PRY filters used the common
default tightening when no explicit multiplier was supplied, while the SCREW
radial restriction used its independently derived task value. Historical runs
are therefore reported as executed and are not regenerated under the later
registry. The registry is treated as declarative specification metadata rather
than as a complete executable fingerprint for these archived runs. Pairwise
comparisons remain margin-matched where both arms use the same bound
construction; PCB and SNAP are interpreted as implemented-governor comparisons
because their compared arms also differ in margin specification.


Every quantitative run records the task constraint fingerprint, margin-policy
hash, outcome-taxonomy hash, and protocol-freeze hash together with execution
mode, evaluated arm, policy algorithm, training seed, selected checkpoint, and
episode seeds. Records with missing or mismatched provenance fields are not
pooled.

The registry is the single binding between environment versions and runtime
arms. The deterministic seed schedule, historical seed audit, specification
and margin audits, and interpretation limits are recorded in
\texttt{docs/provenance.md}. Frozen historical artifacts are retained rather
than rewritten after an audit correction.

The reported training implementation uses Stable-Baselines3 rather than the
retired custom agents. PPO and SAC are the only trained policy algorithms
claimed in the revised paper. Analytical, learned-constraint, oracle, and
matched QP arms are identified by their actual role rather than described as
trained policies.


\paragraph{Protocol-hash lineage.}
Protocol hashes are retained as historical identifiers rather than rewritten
when the protocol record is later amended. The original Stage~1 production

\section{Results \& Discussion}

The central empirical result is a regime distinction: the performance of an
execution governor cannot be interpreted independently of whether the proposal
policy was trained with that governor active. A restriction that is ineffective
when imposed post hoc can become sufficient after the proposal distribution
adapts to the authority it preserves. Conversely, adaptation does not rescue a
restriction that continues to omit task-relevant authority or context.

We therefore organize the results around four questions: what happens when
governance is imposed on a frozen policy; how the same problem changes when the
restriction participates in learning; when representation remains material
after adaptation; and where reactive governance fails because the physical
specification or temporal problem lies outside command projection.

\subsection{Post-hoc Governance of Frozen Policies}

TRiX operates at the execution boundary between an upstream action proposal and
the downstream controller. A proposal is evaluated against the
task-conditioned admissible set and is accepted, projected, rejected, or
declared infeasible before actuation. The experiments below evaluate this
boundary separately from the quality of the policy that generated the
proposal.

Stage~1 asks the most direct runtime-governance question: what happens when a
policy trained without a particular filter is frozen and then executed through
different admissibility mechanisms?

For every task, policy algorithm, and production training seed, one checkpoint
was selected using the frozen validation protocol and then evaluated through
all applicable runtime arms. The paired comparisons reuse that selected
nominal policy and the same held-out episode seed set; the historical exceptions
are identified below. Filtering can change subsequent states and proposals,
so pairing does not imply identical rollout trajectories. Ten independent
training seeds form the experimental units, with 100 held-out episodes per
selected policy/arm in the paired contrasts reported in
Table~\ref{tab:submitted-11}.

\subsubsection{Matched SCREW constraint: analytical methods coincide}

SCREW provides the cleanest control over solution mechanism because the
analytical reference, matched quadratic-program arm, and TRiX are supplied
with the same helical admissibility constraint.

For SAC, \texttt{oracle\_tangent}, \texttt{qp\_matched}, and TRiX each achieve
88.7\% safe completion across the ten production seeds, compared with 0.0\%
for the unfiltered policy. For PPO, the same three constrained arms each
achieve 60.0\%, again compared with 0.0\% unfiltered.

The equality of these outcomes is important. It means the SCREW experiment
does not show that closed-form TRiX projection is safer or more capable than a
matched QP. Given the same admissible-set specification, the correction
mechanisms produce the same task-level result. This does not establish
bitwise equality or a runtime advantage; those claims require a separate
archived numerical benchmark.

\subsubsection{Policy competence limits what a frozen-policy comparison can show}

Stage~1 also exposes a limitation of interpreting runtime-filter results without
examining the upstream policy. PPO failed to acquire competent behavior on
several tasks, so a zero safe-completion rate under a filter does not by itself
identify a failure of the filter.

Likewise, some nominal policies already remain inside the relevant admissible
region, in which case the runtime governor rarely or never binds. CRANK and
PRY also produced uninformative frozen-policy cells under the preregistered
competence criteria. These cases are reported rather than averaged into a
single cross-task ranking.

The paired Stage~1 experiment therefore establishes a narrower point: when a
learned policy requires correction at deployment time, the governor must
represent the relevant admissible authority correctly. Whether that same
representation remains important after the policy has trained under the
restriction is a separate empirical question addressed next.

\subsection{Policy Adaptation Changes the Cost of Governance}

Stage~2 asks a different question from the paired frozen-policy experiment.
Here the evaluated restriction is active during policy training, so the policy
can adapt its nominal proposal distribution to the action authority that the
runtime governor preserves.

PCB provides the clearest illustration of why this distinction matters. In
Stage~1, the componentwise \texttt{box\_clip} arm achieves 0.0\% safe completion
for SAC when imposed on a policy trained without it, even though the same frozen
policy reaches 100.0\% under the structured and analytical correction arms. In
Stage~2, with that componentwise restriction active during learning,
\texttt{box\_clip} reaches 100.0\% safe completion. This comparison does not
depend on the Stage~1 box-versus-TRiX margin asymmetry because it follows the
same componentwise arm across the two training regimes.

The result demonstrates that governor effectiveness is partly a property of
policy-governor co-adaptation. A post-hoc failure does not establish that the
restriction is intrinsically inadequate. Conversely, when a restriction
continues to omit task-relevant context or authority, policy adaptation may
not recover the lost capability.

records across the six benchmark tasks.

\subsubsection{SNAP: phase-conditioned robust governance versus a static baseline}

On SNAP, filter-aware SAC achieves 5.2\% safe completion with the simpler
restriction and 96.5\% with the task-conditioned governor.

For SNAP, this is an implemented-governor comparison rather than a
phase-only or geometry-only ablation. The static arm is phase-blind and uses
nominal bounds, whereas the TRiX arm is phase-conditioned and uses the archived
robust tightening. The observed gap therefore supports the combined governor
configuration but does not isolate phase information or tightening as the sole
cause.

This comparison should not be interpreted as evidence that curved geometry is
responsible for the difference. The phase-conditioned SNAP admissible set is
axis-aligned, and the phase-aware clipping implementation delegates to the
same phase-conditioned projection logic. SNAP is therefore retained as an
implemented-governor result rather than as an ablation isolating either task
state or margin tightening.

The result therefore supports the need for \emph{context-conditioned
admissibility} when safe and useful command authority depends on manipulation
phase.


\subsubsection{CRANK: angle conditioning and task-frame representation vary together}

On CRANK, filter-aware SAC achieves 0.0\% safe completion with the static
restriction and 49.4\% with the task-conditioned governor.

The comparison changes more than one representational factor. The static arm
uses the rotation phase to determine its axial restriction, but its transverse
bounds are fixed in world coordinates using the smaller task-axis authority.
The task-conditioned arm additionally tracks crank angle and expresses the
constraint in the rotating task frame.

Accordingly, the experiment shows that the static representation omits
task-relevant structure, but it does not isolate whether the observed
difference is caused by angle conditioning, the task-frame transform, or their
combination. Nor does the observed training stall prove that a required
tangential command is mathematically excluded at every orientation; it is an
empirical learning outcome under the implemented restriction.


\subsubsection{PCB and PRY: simpler restrictions can be sufficient after adaptation}

The complementary result is equally important. On PCB, filter-aware SAC reaches
100.0\% safe completion with componentwise clipping and 99.8\% with TRiX.
This Stage~2 result does not show a material task-level advantage for exact
structured projection after policy adaptation. It should not be conflated with
the historical Stage~1 PCB comparison, in which filter form and margin
specification differed simultaneously: the componentwise arm bounded each tilt
axis at the nominal $0.100$~N\,m threshold, whereas the executed projection
used a radius of approximately $0.0871$~N\,m, about $12.9\%$ tighter.
Stage~1 therefore does not isolate set geometry as the cause of the observed
PCB difference.

On PRY, filter-aware SAC reaches 88.5\% safe completion with the static
restriction and 89.1\% with TRiX. Once the policy is exposed to the restriction
during training, it can adapt its proposal distribution to the authority
retained by a simpler governor.

A separate PRY control uses a fixed 15,000-step SAC budget, ten training
seeds and 100 evaluation episodes per seed. Its mean safe-completion rates
are 0.0\% without a restriction, 56.0\% with the static restriction and
59.2\% with TRiX; six of ten seeds achieve at least 50\% completion in each
restricted arm. These are separate fixed-budget results, not the Stage~2
checkpoint-selected means above. The restrictions share the intended
zero-insertion bounds but differ as insertion proceeds. The archive's
provenance note reports an initial volume check; without its underlying sample
record we do not retain a numerical volume estimate. The observed results
support the usefulness of both restrictions in this control, without proving
equivalence or isolating task-dependent geometry as a causal factor.


\subsubsection{The training regime is part of the governance problem}

The Stage~1 and Stage~2 results establish that the training regime is part of
the governance problem rather than a nuisance variable. PCB provides the
strongest example because the same componentwise restriction changes from
0.0\% safe completion post hoc to 100.0\% after filter-aware training. SCREW
shows the same interaction more moderately: matched constrained arms achieve
88.7\% safe completion for SAC and 60.0\% for PPO under frozen-policy
evaluation, then reach 100\% for both algorithms when the restriction is active
during training.

This does not imply that adaptation always removes the need for richer
governance. CRANK provides the counterexample: after filter-aware training, the
static restriction remains at 0.0\% while the task-conditioned governor reaches
49.4\%. The combined result is therefore conditional but mechanistic. Richer
governance is most valuable when a simpler restriction omits task-relevant
authority or context that remains necessary for progress. Where the simpler
restriction preserves sufficient authority, policy adaptation can reorganize
the proposal distribution and eliminate an apparent advantage of exact
projection.

\subsection{When Representation Still Matters After Adaptation}

The task-level experiments show that structured governance is sometimes
material and sometimes unnecessary, but individual manipulation tasks do not
by themselves establish a general representational result. We therefore
separate that question from the six disassembly environments using two
complementary analyses: a learned constraint-representation decomposition and
a procedurally generated admissible-set benchmark.


\subsubsection{Learned representation failure is not explained by training-set size}

The PCB decomposition evaluates representations of the admissible set without
treating each representation as a complete trained safety algorithm.

On the held-out proposal set, componentwise clipping yields 2.9\% feasible
commands. A learned action-affine representation yields 0.0\%. A learned
nonlinear representation reaches 78.0\% with one correction step and 92.3\%
when iterated, while the per-action analytical oracle and exact structured
projection each reach 100\%.

The action-affine result remains at 0.0\% feasibility as its training set is
increased from 500 to 20,000 samples. Thus the observed failure of this
specific learned affine model is not removed by supplying more samples from
the same training distribution. The result supports a representational
limitation of that model class in this experiment rather than an
insufficient-data explanation.

The learned nonlinear result also provides an important counterexample to a
simple ``learned versus symbolic'' interpretation: once the representation is
sufficiently expressive, the iterated learned correction makes 92.3\% of the held-out proposals
feasible. This is a proposal-feasibility rate, not a measure of set recovery. Exact projection remains different because it is supplied with
the declared admissible set rather than learning an approximation to it.

The archived run uses seed 0, 20,000 fitting samples and 2,000 test
proposals. Iteration increases feasibility from 78.0\% to 92.3\% in this run.
The result does not establish the same improvement across training seeds.


\subsubsection{Procedural benchmark separates context, approximation, and exact projection}

To test representation independently of the semantics of a named disassembly
task, we evaluated six families of procedurally generated three-dimensional
admissible sets: box, ellipsoid, polytope, state-scaled, rotating, and
phase-gated. The frozen generator produced 120 training, 40 validation, and
200 held-out instances from disjoint seed streams. The learned constraint
models are fitted per evaluated instance, as described in Methods.

Each instance contains a required action sampled from the interior of its exact
admissible set. The held-out instances are partitioned according to whether the
implemented context-free axis-aligned box retains that required action.

This partition requires an explicit qualification. It is a constructed
calibration test: the ``needs structure'' group is defined by the context-free
box excluding the required action. Failure of that same box in this group is
therefore close to definitional and is not presented as independent validation
of the criterion.

The useful empirical information is what the partition does not prescribe:
how alternative representations behave inside each group.

First, the learned affine model retains the required action in 0.0\% of the

capability class: a single learned affine cost representation is not
equivalent to an explicitly represented bounded box, which generally requires
multiple signed inequalities.

Second, context information helps but does not by itself make an approximate
operator exact. The context-conditioned oracle retains the required action in

group. This number measures the authority retained by the implemented
per-context approximation; it should not be interpreted as a generic tangent
projection error rate.

Third, exact projection retains the required action in 100\% of both groups.
This does not establish that exact projection is necessary for every robotic
task---the PCB, PRY, and BATTERY controls show otherwise. It establishes the
narrower representational point that when the required authority lies inside
the declared admissible set, exact access to that set avoids the authority loss
introduced by the evaluated approximations.


\subsubsection{What the combined evidence supports}

Taken together, the learned-representation decomposition and procedural
benchmark narrow the ``Linearity Gap'' claim from the submitted manuscript.
The relevant distinction is not simply linear versus nonlinear mathematics,
nor learned versus symbolic computation. It is whether the governor has the
context, set representation, and solution accuracy required to preserve
task-relevant admissible authority.

An important limitation is built into the procedural stratification. The
``needs structure'' partition is a constructed calibration test: an instance
is placed in that group precisely when the implemented context-free axis-aligned box
excludes its required action. The failure of that same context-free box in
this group is therefore close to definitional and must not be interpreted as
independent validation of the representation criterion. The empirical evidence
comes instead from the behavior of representations that were not used to
define the partition, including the learned affine and nonlinear models, the
context-conditioned oracle, and exact projection, and from their behavior in
the separate ``box suffices'' group.

This interpretation is consistent with the task experiments: simple
restrictions are sufficient on some problems, task-conditioned structure is
material on others, and exact projection cannot compensate for a wrongly
specified physical boundary. The evidence therefore supports a conditional
claim about representation adequacy rather than a universal claim that
structured projection is required.

Future work should test whether the same task-conditioned governance
formulation transfers to other mechanically coupled manipulation problems,
such as peg-in-hole insertion and valve turning; those domains were not
evaluated in the present study.

\subsubsection{Interpretation}

Actual VLM outputs exceed the declared command constraints in these runs,
and downstream governance changes their observed violation outcomes under
paired execution. This provides direct evidence for separating proposal
generation from execution authority. Physical inadmissibility is classified
by command-set membership; it is not automatically a hallucination. The
archive contains no separate semantic-correctness or hallucination annotations.
The experiment measures command governance under its fixed interface and
horizon, with the visual encoding and model provenance stated in Methods.


\subsection{Limitations of Reactive Governance}

The submitted manuscript correctly recognized that reactive command
intervention has boundaries. The revised experiments strengthen that part of
the paper rather than removing it. Four distinct limitations emerge:
accumulated latent state, missing preparatory actions, incorrect constraint
specification, and loss of authority caused by probabilistic tightening. A
fifth prospective experiment is retained precisely because it was
inconclusive.


\subsubsection{Thermal latency: a current command cannot remove accumulated state}

BATTERY v2 explicitly models latent internal-short dynamics that can
continue generating heat after external force is removed. Its acceptance
test applies a damaging command and then sets the command to zero for 2,000
steps, checking that heating continues. This is a property of the declared
model and its implementation test, not independent thermal validation.
We omit precise trajectory temperatures because their supporting trace was
not recovered in the revision archive.

Once this internal state has been created, a governor acting only on the
current external command cannot remove the energy already represented by the
model. This does not mean that preventive runtime intervention is ineffective;
it means that the intervention must occur before the latent failure state is
created.

The matched-specification experiment demonstrates that distinction. When both
the componentwise preventive arm and TRiX enforce the same 15-N preventive
threshold, both reach 100\% safe completion. The earlier apparent difference
between filter forms was caused by comparing different physical thresholds,
not by an unavoidable advantage of one projection operator.


\subsubsection{Sequential constraints: projection cannot invent a missing prerequisite}

SNAP exposes a different limitation. Safe extraction requires latch deflection
before pulling. A reactive governor can suppress a pull command that is
inadmissible in the current latch state, but suppressing that command does not
create the preparatory deflection action required for progress.

This distinction separates command admissibility from task planning. Runtime
projection can enforce the declared action set at a given state; it cannot, by
itself, synthesize a missing action sequence. Addressing that failure mode
requires an upstream planner, temporal specification, or other mechanism that
creates the required precondition.


\subsubsection{Specification failure: exact projection cannot repair the wrong boundary}

Projection correctness and specification correctness are independent.

BATTERY provides the clearest task-level example: enforcing the 20-N flagged
damage threshold and enforcing the 15-N preventive deformation threshold are
different physical specifications. Changing projection geometry cannot make
the former equivalent to the latter.

The sensitivity archive records the expected task-specific ordering in
96 of 105 perturbation cases. Six failed PCB cases reduce stiffness or yield
curvature, or increase the tilt-torque limit, so the permitted torque is
inconsistent with the modeled yield boundary. One failed CRANK case halves
the axial limit, leaving no feasible command under the declared tightening.
The remaining two failures are different: at BATTERY deformation onsets of
22.5 and 30 N, both compared arms have zero destructive completions, but the
designated preventive arm has lower safe completion (12.5\% and 0.0\% versus
100.0\%). They fail the sweep's ordering criterion without recorded damage.

This is a post-hoc description of six PCB, one CRANK and two BATTERY
exceptions. It does not support the earlier claim that all nine failures
were violations of two safety preconditions, or a 96/96 safety guarantee
within a retrospectively defined valid region.


\subsubsection{Probabilistic tightening can eliminate command authority}

CRANK illustrates a failure caused not by the nominal physical constraint but
by the uncertainty allowance applied to it.

For the axial family, the modeled force-noise standard deviation is 1.235 N
and the nominal limit is 5.00 N. The largest feasible Gaussian multiplier is
therefore approximately

\[
z_{\max}=\frac{5.00}{1.235}\approx4.05.
\]

The declared episode-level allowance requires $z=4.488$. The tightened limit
therefore lies below zero, eliminating positive command authority for that
constraint family.

The appropriate conclusion is not to relax the noise model until the
experiment works. The declared tightened command family is infeasible under these
assumptions. Accordingly, the nominal 5-N CRANK configuration is
excluded from enforcement-guarantee claims; its separate representation study
does not require this probabilistic margin and is interpreted on that narrower
basis.


\subsubsection{The prospective BAYONET test was inconclusive}

A separate bayonet task was preregistered to test the representation criterion
prospectively. The protocol declared in advance that the experiment would be
uninformative if no learned arm reached 50\% completion.

That competence gate was not met. Every arm remained below 50\%, with the
highest mean safe-completion rate across ten training seeds reaching
only 10.0\%. Individual seed results are not bounded by that mean. The prospective test
therefore neither confirms nor falsifies the criterion.

The result is retained because the gate and task were frozen before the final
comparison. The task was not softened after the low-competence outcome was
observed. Reporting this null result is part of the evidence that the revised
criterion was not repeatedly modified until every new experiment agreed with
it.

\section{Conclusion}

The principal result of this work is that an execution governor cannot be
evaluated independently of the policy whose proposals it governs. Post-hoc
governance and filter-aware learning are distinct regimes because training with
a restriction changes the proposal distribution presented to that restriction.
On PCB, the same simple componentwise governor changes from 0.0\% safe
completion when imposed post hoc to 100.0\% after filter-aware learning. On
CRANK, adaptation is not sufficient: the static restriction remains at 0.0\%
while the task-conditioned governor reaches 49.4\%. These opposing outcomes
show why neither ``structured projection always wins'' nor ``simple clipping
is sufficient'' is an adequate description.

TRiX provides the architectural setting for studying this interaction by
separating command proposal from command authority. A learned or generative
policy proposes an action, while an explicit admissibility model determines
whether that proposal is accepted, projected, rejected, or declared infeasible
before execution. The revised evidence therefore identifies policy adaptation,
retained action authority, task context, and physical specification as distinct
determinants of runtime-governor behavior.

\subsection{Key Contributions}

The first contribution is the empirical separation between post-hoc governance
and filter-aware learning. A runtime restriction is not evaluated against a
fixed notion of policy behavior: when the restriction participates in
training, the policy can adapt its proposal distribution to the authority that
remains available. PCB provides the clearest example, with the same
componentwise restriction changing from 0.0\% safe completion when imposed
post hoc to 100.0\% after filter-aware training. CRANK provides the complementary
case in which adaptation does not eliminate the observed advantage of the
implemented task-conditioned governor. These results motivate reporting the
two regimes separately rather than treating governor performance as a property
of the filter alone.

The second contribution is the execution-authority formulation itself. TRiX
represents admissible actions as $\mathcal{A}(x,c)$, where the admissible set
may depend on physical state and task context, and places this model between a
command-generating policy and the robot interface. ALLOW, PROJECT, REJECT,
and INFEASIBLE are explicit execution outcomes. The analytical results are
scoped to command admissibility rather than universal closed-loop stability.
On SCREW, analytical projection, matched quadratic-program correction, and
TRiX produce the same task-level result when supplied with the same
admissible-set specification, separating representation from solver choice.

The third contribution is a systematic analysis of representation adequacy.
The rebuilt DISASM-Bench separates policy adaptation, physical specification,
task context, and runtime representation, while the procedural benchmark tests
six admissible-set families on 200 held-out instances. The combined evidence
shows that richer structure is not universally necessary: simple restrictions
can be sufficient when they preserve useful authority and the policy can adapt,
whereas representation and context remain material when simpler restrictions
discard task-relevant admissible actions. The constructed ``needs structure''
partition is treated explicitly as a calibration device rather than as
independent validation.

The fourth contribution extends the execution-boundary analysis to
foundation-model proposals and physical integration. Three vision-language
provider runs use the same 91 images from 28 base configurations, producing
273 decisions. Thirty-five images elicit an inadmissible proposal in at least
one run, and none do so in all three. Across the 257 grounded commands, paired
execution records show 31 constraint-violation cases without governance and
zero with TRiX over 40 steps. The B601-RS deployment provides complementary downstream
evidence for the execution-authority architecture. In the physical intervention
experiment, inadmissible requests were prevented from producing a target and an
excessive \(+50^\circ\) Joint~2 proposal was projected to \(+18^\circ\) before
actuation, with only the projected command physically executed. Three
video-backed trials of the frozen final implementation then completed the
multi-step grasp-and-return sequence with the object retained. The hardware
evidence therefore establishes physical command-boundary intervention and
repeatable task-level execution, while remaining separate from independent
force or wrench calibration.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

The results expose several boundaries of reactive execution governance.
First, command admissibility is only as valid as the physical specification
used to define it. The BATTERY experiments demonstrate this directly:
componentwise and radial restrictions produce the same 100\% safe-completion
outcome when both enforce the same 15-N preventive threshold, whereas the
earlier comparison mixed a 20-N flagged threshold with a 15-N preventive
threshold. Exact projection therefore cannot compensate for a wrongly chosen
physical boundary.

Second, reactive correction does not remove latent physical state. In the
BATTERY model, the internal-short state can continue producing heat
after the external command returns to zero. Similarly, a reactive governor cannot
supply a missing task prerequisite, such as a latch-deflection action that
must precede extraction. These cases motivate combining command-level
governance with predictive or hierarchical mechanisms when future state or
action sequence determines admissibility.

Third, probabilistic margins are conditional on their modeling assumptions
and can eliminate all useful control authority. For the reported CRANK
configuration, the declared chance-constraint tightening exceeds the maximum
feasible value for the axial-force family. That configuration is therefore
excluded from enforcement-guarantee claims. More generally, satisfying the
implemented constraint model is necessary for the claims made here but is not
sufficient to establish safety under omitted dynamics, tracking error,
perception error, incorrect thresholds, or unmodeled contact phenomena.

Fourth, learned-policy competence limits what a governance experiment can
establish. Several frozen-policy cells are uninformative because the nominal
policy does not acquire the task, and the preregistered bayonet stress test
did not meet its predeclared competence gate. These outcomes are reported
rather than repaired after observation. Future studies should therefore
separate governor capability from the ability of the upstream policy to
generate task-relevant proposals.

The foundation-model experiment evaluates one proposed command per image
and paired governance over 40 simulator steps. Its 91 images contain repeated
conditions from 28 base configurations. The original visual encoding scales
the mechanical tilt; its positive main-stratum scale preserves ordering but
does not calibrate apparent image distance to the mechanical boundary.
Configured model aliases are recorded, while resolved backend IDs, access
times and trial-level retry histories are unavailable. The comparison therefore
characterizes the recorded runs rather than pinned-version model rankings.
The measured benefit is prevented constraint-violation cases. Establishing
physical damage prevention, calibrated visual-state understanding or autonomous
task completion would require measurements of those outcomes.

\subsubsection{Simulation-to-Hardware Gap}

The B601-RS experiments deliberately evaluate a narrower physical claim than
the simulation studies. The archived intervention experiment establishes that
\texttt{ALLOW}, \texttt{PROJECT}, and \texttt{REJECT} decisions can determine
which physical target reaches the robot. The three video-backed confirmation
trials establish repeatable task-level execution under the frozen final command
path.

The grasp experiment does not evaluate autonomous perception or grasp
synthesis. Object placement is calibrated, and the hardware executor includes
the disclosed Joint~2 grasp-ready adjustment and Joint~7 gripper calibration.
These are implementation mappings for the physical primitive rather than
learned grasp generation.

No independent force/torque sensor is used. The hardware evidence therefore
does not establish that a modeled force or wrench boundary corresponds to the
true contact boundary of the robot-object system. Independent wrench
measurement, broader object variation, and evaluation under perception and
contact uncertainty remain future validation tasks.

\subsection{Broader Impact}

Robotic disassembly provides a useful setting for execution governance because
successful operation requires both task progress and respect for
component-specific physical constraints. The same architectural question also
arises whenever a high-level learned system can generate commands more freely
than the physical system should execute them: who retains final command
authority, and how can that decision be inspected after execution?

TRiX addresses that question by making the admissibility decision explicit
and recordable. This structure can improve interpretability of intervention
events and can support post-hoc auditing of why a command was accepted,
modified, rejected, or declared infeasible. The present experiments do not
establish regulatory compliance or general transfer to other robotic domains,
but they motivate further study of execution-governance interfaces in systems
where learned proposal generation and physical command authority should remain
separate.

\subsection{Conclusion}

The revised evidence supports a mechanistic rather than a universal claim.
Execution-governor performance depends jointly on the authority retained by
the restriction and on whether the proposal policy has adapted to that
restriction. Post-hoc evaluation and filter-aware learning should therefore be
reported separately rather than pooled into a single notion of ``safety-layer
performance.'' When a simpler restriction preserves sufficient task authority,
adaptation can make it effectively sufficient. When it removes task-essential
authority or lacks required context, structured governance can remain material
even after training.

TRiX therefore contributes an explicit neuro-symbolic boundary between
proposed robot actions and executable commands, together with an evaluation
framework for studying that boundary under different proposal distributions.
The foundation-model study reinforces the same architectural point from a
different direction: shared images elicit different inadmissible proposal
sets across actual VLM runs, and TRiX prevents all 31 observed
constraint-violation cases across 257 paired command executions during the
40-step horizon. The broader lesson is
that learned proposal generation, runtime admissibility, and physical command
authority are coupled components that should be evaluated as such.

\section{Admissibility definitions and scope}

The revised theoretical analysis does not require the nominal controller
stability and bounded-neural-error assumptions used by the submitted ISS
argument. Instead, the following definitions specify the conditions under
which the command-level governor is interpreted.

\paragraph{Declared task context.}
The admissible action set is written as
$\mathcal{A}(x,c)$, where $x$ denotes the observed physical state and $c$
denotes task context such as primitive identity, phase, or task-frame
orientation. Any result involving context-conditioned admissibility assumes
that the corresponding context variable is supplied to the governor. The implemented context-limited runtime arms are identified by the
observation fields used to construct their bounds. In the benchmark
harness, sharing the same task observation does not require every arm to
condition on every field; the concrete field use is specified in
Appendix D.

\paragraph{Projection well-posedness.}
Whenever TRiX returns a projected command, the corresponding admissible set is
required to be nonempty and closed so that a Euclidean minimizer exists. When
the set used in an experiment is convex, the minimizer is unique. For
non-projectable symbolic violations, the runtime outcome is REJECT rather than
an implicit projection onto an undefined set.

\paragraph{Specification validity.}
Membership in $\mathcal{A}(x,c)$ means satisfaction of the \emph{modeled}
command-level constraint. It does not prove that the specified threshold,
margin, coordinate transform, or physical model is correct. Specification
validity is therefore evaluated separately from solver correctness. This
distinction is central to the BATTERY matched-specification experiment and the
PCB margin audit in the revised study.

\paragraph{Execution scope.}
The analytical results concern command admissibility and representation. They
do not establish closed-loop stability, forward invariance of an unmodeled
plant, or robustness to arbitrary state-estimation, tracking, perception, or
contact-model error. Those effects are outside the theorem-level claims of the
revised manuscript.

\section{Reference Projection Operators}

This appendix records the analytical operators used in the revised benchmark.
The revised framework is expressed in terms of admissible action sets rather
than assuming that every task constraint is an equality manifold.


\subsection{Helical Projection (SCREW)}

SCREW v3 uses normalized command
$a=(a_v,a_\omega,a_r)$, with
$v_z^c=S_v a_v$, $\omega_z^c=S_\omega a_\omega$ and
$F_r^c=S_r a_r$. The constants are
$S_v=0.05$ m\,s$^{-1}$, $S_\omega=60$ rad\,s$^{-1}$,
$S_r=40$ N, $p=1.25$ mm, $k=p/(2\pi)$ and
$\epsilon=0.001$ m\,s$^{-1}$. The implemented command set is
\[
\mathcal A_{\rm screw}=
\left\{a:
\begin{array}{l}
|S_v a_v-kS_\omega a_\omega|\leq\epsilon-\eta,\\
|a_v|\leq1,\quad |a_\omega|\leq1,\quad |a_r|\leq r_{\rm int}
\end{array}\right\},
\]
where $\eta=10^{-6}\epsilon$ is the numerical interior offset and
$r_{\rm int}$ is the radial bound after the recorded stochastic margin.

The axial--angular slice is a convex polygon obtained by clipping the command
square with the two helical halfspaces. An admissible proposal is unchanged;
otherwise the analytical implementation selects the nearest point on the
polygon's edges. Radial action is clipped independently. The objective is
Euclidean distance in normalized action coordinates.

The matched QP minimizes that same objective over these same inequalities.
The zero-tolerance line projection in the main text illustrates the coupling;
it is not the finite-slab operator used in the experiments. Equal task-level
SCREW results do not by themselves certify pointwise numerical equality.


\subsection{Disk--Interval Projection (PCB)}

PCB v2 uses normalized action

\[
a=
\begin{bmatrix}
a_x & a_y & a_z
\end{bmatrix}^{\top},
\]

where the first two components command tilt torque and the third commands lift.
Let $r_\tau$ denote the declared tilt radius in normalized action units and let
$[\ell_z,r_z]$ denote the allowed lift interval. The admissible set is

\[
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{PCB}}
=
\left\{
a:
a_x^2+a_y^2\leq r_\tau^2,
\;
\ell_z\leq a_z\leq r_z
\right\}.
\]

Writing

\[
\rho=\sqrt{a_x^2+a_y^2},
\]

the exact Euclidean projection of the tilt pair is

\[
(a_x',a_y')
=
\begin{cases}
(a_x,a_y), & \rho\leq r_\tau,\\[4pt]
\dfrac{r_\tau}{\rho}(a_x,a_y), & \rho>r_\tau,
\end{cases}
\]

and the lift component is

\[
a_z'=\min\{r_z,\max\{\ell_z,a_z\}\}.
\]

Thus the revised PCB operator projects onto a disk crossed with an interval.
It does \emph{not} zero both tilt torques, and it is not the six-dimensional
wrench operator described in the submitted appendix.

The physical nominal planarity limit is
$\tau_{\max}=0.100\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. Where chance tightening is enabled, the
runtime radius is derived separately from the margin policy rather than being
silently substituted for the physical threshold.


\subsection{BATTERY Specification and Runtime Operators}

The submitted appendix represented BATTERY as projection onto the instantaneous
half-space $F\cdot v\leq P_{\max}(T)$. That expression is not used as the
sole enforcement model in BATTERY v2.

The revised task distinguishes preventive deformation limits, modeled damage
onset, internal short, and subsequent thermal evolution. Runtime comparisons
therefore identify both the \emph{constraint specification} and the
\emph{operator form}. In particular, the matched-specification experiment
compares componentwise and TRiX operators using the same 15-N preventive
threshold. The submitted comparison that mixed a 20-N damage threshold with a
different projection geometry is retained only as provenance and is not used
to infer representational superiority.

The executable definitions of the BATTERY operators are fingerprinted with the
benchmark registry and margin policy. Because the scientific comparison
depends on specification as well as projection form, we do not replace those
definitions here with a second informal analytical approximation.


\subsection{Phase- and Frame-Conditioned Operators}

SNAP and CRANK use task-conditioned admissible sets. In SNAP, the applicable
pull authority depends on latch state and manipulation phase. In CRANK, the
transverse admissible set additionally depends on task-frame orientation.

These operators are evaluated specifically as context-conditioned runtime
governors. Their purpose is not to establish that curved geometry is generally
superior to clipping; the experiments test whether the information and action
authority required by the task are available to the governor.


\subsection{PRY Restriction}

PRY provides a control case in which a simpler restriction can retain the
task-relevant authority. The separate fixed-budget control compares the
implemented static and insertion-dependent restrictions. Similar observed
completion rates do not establish equivalence or a geometric causal effect;
the reported outcome is scoped to those policies and configurations.

\section{Supplementary proofs for representation propositions}

This appendix consolidates the representation results stated in Section~3.
They characterize properties of admissible action sets and do not constitute a
closed-loop stability theorem.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{Proposition 1: separable sets reduce to componentwise clipping}

Let

\[
\mathcal{B}
=
\prod_{i=1}^{m}[\ell_i,r_i]
\]

and consider the Euclidean projection

\[
\Pi_{\mathcal{B}}(\tilde u)
=
\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal{B}}
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(u_i-\tilde u_i)^2 .
\]

Because both the objective and feasible set separate by coordinate, the
optimization decomposes into $m$ scalar problems,

\[
u_i^\star
=
\arg\min_{\ell_i\leq u_i\leq r_i}
\frac{1}{2}(u_i-\tilde u_i)^2 .
\]

Each scalar minimizer is

\[
u_i^\star
=
\min\{r_i,\max\{\ell_i,\tilde u_i\}\},
\]

which is exactly componentwise clipping. Therefore an exact Euclidean
projector has no representational advantage over clipping when both methods
enforce the same axis-aligned product set with the same margins.

This result does not apply when the admissible set contains coupled
coordinates, state dependence, phase dependence, or a different physical
margin.


\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{Proposition 2: consequence of phase blindness}

Let $\mathcal{A}_1$ and $\mathcal{A}_2$ denote the admissible sets in two task
phases and let $\mathcal{B}$ be a single phase-independent restriction.

For $\mathcal{B}$ to be admissible in phase 1,

\[
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_1,
\]

and for it to be admissible in phase 2,

\[
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}_2.
\]

Hence

\[
\mathcal{B}
\subseteq
\mathcal{A}_1\cap\mathcal{A}_2 .
\]

If $\mathcal{A}_1\neq\mathcal{A}_2$, their intersection is a strict subset of
at least one phase-specific set. Thus any restriction that is safe in both
phases necessarily removes some phase-specific admissible authority from at
least one phase. Conversely, if a phase-independent restriction admits an
action outside the intersection, that action is inadmissible in at least one
phase.

The proposition establishes a representation/information limitation only. It
does not establish that the removed actions are required for successful task
completion. Whether the lost authority is task-essential is determined
empirically.


\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{Proposition 3: conservatism under unknown rotation}

Let

\[
\mathcal{R}_0
=
\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:
|u_1|\leq a,\;
|u_2|\leq b\},
\qquad
a\geq b>0,
\]

and let

\[
\mathcal{R}_{\theta}
=
R(\theta)\mathcal{R}_0 .
\]

First consider any $u$ satisfying $\lVert u\rVert_2\leq b$. Rotation preserves
Euclidean norm, so in every rotated coordinate system each component has
magnitude at most $\lVert u\rVert_2\leq b\leq a$. Hence

\[
u\in\mathcal{R}_{\theta}
\quad
\text{for every }\theta.
\]

Therefore the radius-$b$ disk is contained in
$\bigcap_{\theta}\mathcal{R}_{\theta}$.

Now let $\lVert u\rVert_2>b$. Choose an orientation for which the narrow axis
of the rectangle is aligned with $u$. In that orientation, the narrow-axis
coordinate of $u$ has magnitude $\lVert u\rVert_2>b$, so

\[
u\notin\mathcal{R}_{\theta}.
\]

Therefore

\[
\bigcap_{\theta}\mathcal{R}_{\theta}
=
\{u:\lVert u\rVert_2\leq b\}.
\]

For a symmetric fixed world-frame box $[-h,h]^2$, the corner
$(h,h)$ has norm $\sqrt{2}h$. Requiring every point in the box to remain
inside the radius-$b$ disk gives

\[
\sqrt{2}h\leq b,
\]

and hence

\[
h\leq\frac{b}{\sqrt{2}}.
\]

This is a worst-case orientation result. The CRANK experiment exercises
angle-conditioned task-frame constraints, but is not interpreted as an
experimental isolation of rotation alone.


\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{Proposition 4: feasibility of probabilistic tightening}

Let the nominal scalar physical limit be

\[
|y|\leq L,
\qquad
L>0,
\]

and let uncertainty have standard deviation $\sigma>0$. For a declared
Gaussian quantile $z>0$, the tightening margin is

\[
m=z\sigma.
\]

The tightened admissible interval is

\[
|y|\leq L-z\sigma.
\]

Its radius is therefore

\[
r=L-z\sigma.
\]

Three cases follow directly:

\begin{enumerate}
\item If $z\sigma<L$, then $r>0$ and the tightened set contains nonzero
      admissible commands.
\item If $z\sigma=L$, then $r=0$ and the only admissible command is $y=0$.
\item If $z\sigma>L$, then $r<0$ and the tightened interval is empty.
\end{enumerate}

Thus nonzero command authority exists if and only if

\[
z<\frac{L}{\sigma}.
\]

This result separates probabilistic-margin feasibility from the behavior of
the projection algorithm. A projection mechanism cannot recover authority
that has been eliminated by the declared margin.


\subsection*{Affine projection and quadratic-program equivalence}

For completeness, consider the affine constraint

\[
Au=b
\]

with full-row-rank $A$. The Euclidean projection problem

\[
\min_u
\frac{1}{2}\lVert u-\tilde u\rVert_2^2
\quad
\text{subject to}
\quad
Au=b
\]

has Lagrangian

\[
\mathcal{L}(u,\lambda)
=
\frac{1}{2}\lVert u-\tilde u\rVert_2^2
+
\lambda^\top(Au-b).
\]

Stationarity gives

\[
u-\tilde u+A^\top\lambda=0,
\]

so

\[
u=\tilde u-A^\top\lambda.
\]

Substitution into the equality constraint yields

\[
AA^\top\lambda=A\tilde u-b,
\]

and therefore

\[
u^\star
=
\tilde u
-
A^\top(AA^\top)^{-1}(A\tilde u-b).
\]

Accordingly, a closed-form implementation and a quadratic-program solver
provided with the same affine constraint solve the same mathematical
optimization problem. The revised SCREW solver comparison tests numerical
agreement and computational cost, not representational superiority.


\subsection*{Policy adaptation}

No proposition in this appendix states that training can or cannot overcome a
particular runtime restriction. Filter-aware training changes the policy's
proposal distribution and may allow the policy to adapt to retained action
authority. Whether that adaptation succeeds is task dependent and is therefore
reported only as an empirical result in Section~5.

The taxonomy separates the role of a measured constraint from the physical
mechanism that generated it. Outcome-affecting families enter the five-category
episode contract, whereas diagnostic families remain visible without changing
an intact completion by themselves. An unclassified family defaults to
outcome-affecting so that adding a new constraint cannot silently remove it
from evaluation.

One frozen naming mismatch is retained for provenance. The SNAP environment
emits \texttt{vio\_premature}, whereas the explicit taxonomy entry is named
\texttt{vio\_premature\_pull}. Because unlisted violation families inherit the
outcome-affecting default, the emitted family has the intended classification.
We preserve the historical taxonomy hash rather than silently changing the
declaration after the production runs.


\subsection{Chance-Constraint Margins}

Where stochastic tightening is used, the margin is derived from an
episode-level risk target and the applicable episode horizon. With Gaussian
noise this is a chance constraint under the assumed distribution, not a
deterministic robustness guarantee.

The implementation records separately whether a tightened set is

\begin{enumerate}
\item nonempty,
\item nonempty with positive nonzero command authority, or
\item infeasible.
\end{enumerate}

This distinction is required because the singleton set containing only the zero
command is mathematically nonempty while still providing no nonzero authority
for task progress.

\paragraph{Training and held-out use.}
The production training seeds are $0,\ldots,9$. The current deterministic
selection scheduler uses separate validation and test seed bases of
$500{,}000$ and $800{,}000$, respectively, plus fixed task offsets. The
historical seed-pairing exceptions and their treatment remain as documented
in the reproducibility section. Held-out evaluation changes the seed stream,
not the parameter ranges or noise distribution. It is therefore an
in-distribution held-out evaluation, not a widened out-of-distribution
parameter sweep. The separate constant-sensitivity study is not part of this
reset-time randomisation specification, and the submitted wider random-action
sweep is not the primary revised evaluation.

The constraint identifiers above match the frozen v2/v3 modules. They do not
hash the complete observation and reset implementations. Reproducing those
interfaces additionally requires the corresponding source revision, including
the reset distributions and observation mappings recorded here.

\section{Seed-Level Statistical Analysis}

The training seed is the experimental unit for learned-policy comparisons.
Each arm/seed estimate in Table~\ref{tab:submitted-11} uses 100 held-out
episodes. Episode RNG streams within a policy are not treated as independent
policy replicates. The submitted Welch-test table based on three evaluation
RNG streams is withdrawn.

For each contrast, $d_i$ is the difference in safe-completion percentage
between the two arms at training-seed identifier $i$, for $i=0,\ldots,9$.
We report $\bar d$ and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of its empirical
bootstrap distribution, resampling the ten seed pairs with replacement.
Because the per-seed percentages are integral at 100 episodes, this finite
bootstrap distribution is computed exactly by ten convolutions of the
empirical distribution of $d_i$, without Monte Carlo sampling. These are
descriptive intervals reconstructed from the archived counts; they are not
multiplicity-adjusted hypothesis tests and do not separately resample episodes.

Stage~1 pairs reuse a selected frozen policy. Stage~2 pairs and the PCB
cross-regime contrast compare separately trained policies matched by
training-seed identifier. The archived preventive BATTERY arms use different
held-out episode seed sets (850000--850099 versus 845000--845099), as do the
PCB filter-aware and post-hoc box arms. PCB's archived episode ranges also
vary across training seeds; the companion per-seed data retain the exact
lists. These contrasts are therefore paired at the training-seed level only.
All other reported contrasts were checked against matching episode seed sets.
The audit reconciles 220 aggregate records with 4400 evaluation shards and
all 80 archived Figure~\ref{fig:seed-distributions} points. Historical
Stage~1 BATTERY results remain outside this table.

\section{Implementation and Specification Validation}

The revised validation separates implementation agreement from independent
physical validation.

\paragraph{Implementation consistency.}
Environment acceptance tests verify declared state transitions, outcome
families, progress metrics, constraint fingerprints, and filter behavior.
Agreement between code and the equations used to construct that same
environment establishes implementation consistency only.

\paragraph{Matched solver specification.}
The analytical, active-set QP and operator-splitting implementations in
\texttt{baselines/screw\_solvers.py} use the same normalized SCREW command
polytope. The policy results establish matching task-level outcomes for the
registered analytical and matched-QP arms. The archived evidence does not
support retaining a specific pointwise error bound in this draft.

\paragraph{Specification sensitivity.}
The archived sweep contains 21 constants at five multipliers, with eight
scripted episodes per arm and case. It records 96 passing and nine failed
task-specific orderings. The failures comprise six PCB boundary/model
inconsistencies, one CRANK tightened-set failure, and two BATTERY cases with
zero destructive completions but lower safe completion for the designated
preventive arm. These are post-hoc diagnoses of the observed sweep, not a
96/96 certification conditional on two sufficient safety preconditions.

\section{Computational Scope}

The source provides an analytical polygon projector, an active-set QP and a
reused operator-splitting QP for the same SCREW command specification. This
separates the mathematical set from the choice of numerical implementation.
No per-action latency comparison or speedup is reported here because the
previously quoted timing pair could not be tied to an archived matched
benchmark record. The observed equality of task-level outcomes is retained;
it does not establish a computational-efficiency advantage.
