\begin{abstract}
Recovering materials from electronic waste requires robots to take products
apart without damaging the parts inside. Robots now receive candidate actions
from learned policies and foundation models, so something must decide which
reach the motors. Here we show that the value of such an execution
governor depends on the policy it governs. We present TRiX, which passes,
corrects or refuses each proposed command using constraints tied to the task's
current state, and evaluate it across simulation, real vision-language-model
outputs and physical hardware. A simple filter that permits no safe extraction
when added to a trained policy permits safe extraction every time when present
during training, while task information missing from a filter cannot be
learned around. Governance intercepts every recorded constraint violation in
model commands and corrects commands on a physical arm before execution.
Outside training conditions, correctly enforced constraints still permit
latch damage, marking the limit of per-step governance. Governors must be
evaluated with the policies they serve.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} robotic disassembly; execution governance; action
projection; robot learning; vision-language models

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Electronic waste is among the fastest-growing waste streams, and recovering
its materials depends on taking products apart without destroying what is
inside \cite{ref1,ref2,ref3,ref4,ref5,ref6}. Automating that work is difficult
because each operation is governed by physical relationships the robot cannot
simply see. A screw must turn as it withdraws, or its thread strips. A latch
must be pressed aside before a cover will lift. A battery pressed too hard can
short internally and keep heating after the robot lets go. Whether a command
is acceptable depends on these relationships and on where the task currently
stands.

Robots now have far richer sources of candidate actions. Learned policies and
vision-language models (VLMs) can propose manipulation commands from images,
instructions and experience \cite{ref8,ref9,ref10,ref11,ref12,ref13}, and
their roles in reasoning, planning and interaction for collaborative robotics
are developing quickly \cite{ref39}. These proposers widen what a robot can
attempt, but nothing in them guarantees that a proposed command respects the
physics of the part in front of it. A model can describe the right step and
still command a pull where a turn was needed. Something must decide which
proposals are allowed to reach the motors.

Established methods place that decision in different locations
(Table~\ref{tab:submitted-1}). Constrained reinforcement learning shapes the
policy itself under cost limits \cite{ref16,ref17}, and Lagrangian methods
adjust penalties as learning proceeds \cite{ref18,ref19,ref20}. Safety layers
correct actions through a learned constraint model \cite{ref21}, recovery
policies take over when estimated risk is high \cite{ref22}, and control
barrier functions enforce state-dependent safe sets under explicit dynamical
and feasibility assumptions \cite{ref23,ref24}, with extensions to higher
relative degree \cite{ref40} and time-varying constraints \cite{ref41}. When
such a mechanism acts as a runtime filter, two factors outside the filter
shape what it achieves. The first is what it is told: which constraints it
represents, and which task information, such as the current phase or the
orientation of the part, it can use. The second is when the policy meets it.
A filter added to an already trained policy must correct proposals that were
never shaped by it, whereas a policy trained with the filter in place can
learn to work within it. A filter that looks inadequate in one setting can be
sufficient in the other.

Two kinds of failure matter in this setting. Battery deformation that
progresses to an internal short and heating is a hazard; a fractured circuit
board, a stripped thread or a broken latch is a loss of asset integrity. TRiX
enforces the constraints of declared physical models of both, and our
measurements report command admissibility and task outcomes under those
models. Physical safety for a specific product additionally requires that its
models be calibrated against measured forces, a step the framework makes
explicit.

This paper studies both factors through TRiX, an execution governor placed
between the proposer and the robot (Figure~\ref{fig:submitted-1}). For each
proposed command, TRiX uses the current task state and declared task context
to select the set of commands the task model admits. It passes a command
already in that set, projects one outside it onto the nearest admissible
command, refuses one that cannot be meaningfully corrected, and reports when
the specification leaves no usable command at all. Only passed or corrected
commands reach the executor. Because the governor is explicit about what it
represents and when it acts, the same governor can be evaluated in both
settings: applied to a policy after training, and present while the policy
learns.

We evaluate TRiX on three foundations, each closer to deployment than the
last. In simulation, DISASM-Bench provides six analytical disassembly tasks,
screw removal, circuit-board extraction, snap-fit release, crank removal,
battery peeling and prying, on which reinforcement-learning policies are
trained across ten seeds with and without the governor. With foundation
models, commands proposed by three VLMs from shared images of the
circuit-board task are replayed with and without governance from identical
states. On hardware, a physical robot arm runs the governor in its command
path, so that rejections and corrections change what the motors actually
execute. Together these layers test one execution contract against learned
proposers, generative proposers and real actuation. The Methods describe the
contract and each layer in turn, and the Results follow the same order, from
simulation to foundation-model commands to hardware.

The results show that a governor cannot be judged apart from the policy it
serves. On circuit-board extraction, the same simple restriction yields
0.0\% safe completion when added to a trained policy and 100.0\% when present
during training. Learning does not fill every gap: on crank removal, where the
admissible forces turn with the part, a fixed restriction stays at 0.0\%
while the task-conditioned governor reaches 49.4\%. When analytical projection
and a quadratic program are given the same constraint on screw removal, their
outcomes are identical, which separates how a correction is computed from what
the governor is told. Outside the conditions seen in training, snap-fit safe
completion under TRiX falls from 96.5\% to 59.9\%, and latches fracture even
though every executed command stays within its bounds, which locates precisely
where per-step governance must be extended. For commands proposed by VLMs,
governance prevents all 31 observed constraint-violation cases without
introducing any, and on the robot arm it rejects and corrects commands before
they are executed. Together, these findings establish execution governance as
an explicit contract whose value is measured jointly with the proposer, the
specification and the task context.

\section{Methods}

The Methods follow the path of a command. We first define the execution
contract that every proposal passes through, then the three layers on which it
is evaluated: the simulation benchmark with its policies, comparators and
stress tests; the recorded foundation-model commands; and the physical
platform. A final section covers measurement, statistics and provenance.

\subsection{The execution contract}
\label{sec:authority}
TRiX receives a proposed command $\tilde a$ together with the observed state
$x$ and a declared task context $c$, such as the current phase of a snap-fit
release. From these it selects the admissible command set $\mathcal A(x,c)$
and returns one of four outcomes. \texttt{ALLOW} forwards a proposal already
in the set. \texttt{PROJECT} replaces an inadmissible proposal with a feasible
correction. \texttt{REJECT} refuses a symbolic or task-state violation that no
correction can repair, and \texttt{INFEASIBLE} reports that the selected
specification supplies no usable command authority. Only the first two forward
a command for execution. When $\mathcal A(x,c)$ is a nonempty closed convex
set in normalized command coordinates, the correction is the unique Euclidean
projection
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathcal A}(\tilde a)=\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal A(x,c)}
\tfrac12\|a-\tilde a\|_2^2.
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
The normalization is itself part of the model: velocity, angular-velocity and
force errors share this metric only after each has a declared scale.

Screw removal shows why the representation of $\mathcal A$ matters. A screw
of pitch $p$ advances one pitch per turn, so ideal motion couples axial
velocity $v_z$ to angular velocity $\omega_z$ through $v_z=k\omega_z$, where
$k=p/(2\pi)$. In normalized commands $a_v=v_z/S_v$ and
$a_\omega=\omega_z/S_\omega$, with coupling coefficient
$\alpha=kS_\omega/S_v$, projection onto the ideal line is
\begin{equation}
\Pi_0(\tilde a)=
\frac{\alpha\tilde a_v+\tilde a_\omega}{1+\alpha^2}
\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:helix}
\end{equation}
No pair of independent bounds on $a_v$ and $a_\omega$ can express this
coupling. The implemented SCREW task widens the line into a finite-tolerance
strip that occupies 2.0\% of the normalized command slice in
Figure~\ref{fig:submitted-2}a, bounded by command and radial-force limits.
Componentwise clipping can leave a proposal outside that strip, whereas
projection lands on it (Figure~\ref{fig:submitted-2}b). A suitably specified
linear-constraint quadratic program (QP) represents the same set, which lets
the experiments separate the solution mechanism from the constraint
representation.

Four properties of command sets frame the experiments. Projection onto an
axis-aligned product of intervals reduces to componentwise clipping. A
phase-independent set valid in two phases must lie within their intersection
and may therefore discard authority that one phase allows. The intersection
of a rectangle with half-widths $a\geq b>0$ over all rotations is the disk of
radius $b$, so a fixed square $[-h,h]^2$ valid at every orientation requires
$h\leq b/\sqrt2$. A Gaussian-tightened interval $|y|\leq L-z\sigma$ keeps
nonzero authority exactly when $z\sigma<L$. \suppsection{S1},
\suppsection{S2} and \suppsection{S3} give the assumptions and proofs. These
properties describe what a command set can represent and when it retains
authority; closed-loop stability and forward invariance for an unmodeled plant
require dynamical analysis beyond this contract. How a trained policy adapts
to a restriction is measured empirically rather than stated as a proposition.

\subsection{Simulation benchmark}
\label{sec:tasks}
DISASM-Bench turns six common disassembly operations into analytical models
with declared constraints and damage mechanisms: SCREW (screw removal), PCB
(circuit-board extraction), SNAP (snap-fit release), CRANK (crank removal),
BATTERY (battery peeling) and PRY (prying a bonded part open with a lever).
Each task maps three normalized action coordinates to its own physical
interface (Table~\ref{tab:submitted-2}). SCREW commands axial and angular
velocity $v_z^c,\omega_z^c$ and radial force $F_r$; PCB commands tilt torques
$\tau_x,\tau_y$ and lift force $F_z$; SNAP commands latch-deflection, pull and
lateral forces $F_{\rm def},F_{\rm pull},F_{\rm lat}$; CRANK commands forces
$F_x,F_y,F_z$; BATTERY commands peel force $F_{\rm peel}$ and lateral forces
$F_{\ell1},F_{\ell2}$; and PRY commands tool torque $\tau$, insertion force
$F_{\rm ins}$ and lateral force $F_{\rm lat}$, with a torque limit that
depends on insertion depth $d$.

A versioned registry binds SCREW v3 and the five v2 tasks to their
environments and named filters. Every observation has ten float32 entries
whose meanings are task-specific, with unused entries padded with zeros; PCB,
for example, observes lift and lift velocity, two tilt angles and their rates,
accumulated damage and a fracture flag. Actions are normalized to $[-1,1]^3$
and mapped through the physical scales in \suppsection{S4}. Each outer action
advances $1/240$~s of simulated time, integrated with 40 internal substeps for
SCREW, ten for PCB, SNAP, CRANK and BATTERY, and directly for PRY. Progress
and termination are task-specific.

Every episode ends in one outcome defined by a single contract. Safe
completion requires successful task termination without the task's declared
outcome-affecting violation. The contract separates it from completion with
such a violation, from destructive completion and from the remaining
unsuccessful outcomes, and it records diagnostic events separately. Reset
parameters, their distributions and ranges, and whether each is used for
training or evaluation are tabulated in \suppref{tab:submitted-9} and
detailed in \suppsection{S4.6}; risk allocations, noise and reset variation
all come from the frozen source.

illustrated gripper shows the command interface, while arm and contact

\subsection{Policies, regimes and comparators}
\label{sec:training-methods}
We train two standard reinforcement-learning algorithms, Proximal Policy
Optimization (PPO) \cite{ref31} and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) \cite{ref32},
with Stable-Baselines3 2.9.0, Gymnasium 1.3.0 and two-layer $[256,256]$
networks on CPU, using ten independent training seeds, 0--9, for every
comparison. The two evaluation regimes mirror the two ways a governor can meet
a policy. In Stage~1, a policy is trained without the evaluated governor, a
checkpoint is selected, and each runtime arm in Table~\ref{tab:submitted-3} is
applied to that same frozen policy. In Stage~2, a separate policy is trained
with its restriction inside the environment transition, while replay and
rollout buffers retain the nominal policy action. The training adapter
separates termination from time-limit truncation and restores NumPy random
state after interleaved evaluation.

A predeclared rule selects checkpoints so that no arm benefits from tuning.
At each task budget, checkpoints saved every 1,000 steps are screened on 30
fixed validation episodes. Three-checkpoint windows are ranked by mean safe
completion, and the centers of the three best windows receive 70 additional
validation episodes. Selection is lexicographic: highest safe completion, then
lowest destructive completion, highest normalized progress and lowest
episode-level violation incidence, with the earliest checkpoint breaking any
remaining tie. Each selected policy is tested on 100 held-out episodes
disjoint from validation, and pilot seeds are excluded. Checkpoint lists,
software fingerprints, task budgets and episode-seed exceptions are retained
in \suppsection{S5} and \suppsection{S6}.

The runtime arms are chosen so that each comparison changes one factor where
possible and states what it changes where not (Table~\ref{tab:submitted-3}).
Componentwise restrictions, analytical local-gradient references, a matched
QP and the task-specific TRiX operators form the registry. The quantitative
policy matrix contains PPO and SAC task policies; no trained PPO-Lagrangian or
complete trained SafeLayer policy is part of it, and learned constraint
representations are studied separately below. The literature methods in
Table~\ref{tab:submitted-1} keep their original assumptions and are
positioned conceptually rather than run as experimental baselines.

Two controls hold the specification fixed. For SCREW, the matched QP and the

construction. For BATTERY, the preventive arms share the 15-N threshold and

and CRANK combines frame and angle conditioning. Historical BATTERY prefix

\subsection{Representation, shift and sensitivity studies}
\label{sec:model-validation}
Four studies stress the benchmark beyond the main policy comparisons. Two
examine constraint representation directly, apart from trained policies. The
PCB decomposition uses the archived seed-0 run, fitting learned models on
20,000 samples and testing them on 2,000 held-out proposals, together with an
affine-model sweep over 500--20,000 fitting samples. The procedural benchmark
generates six families of three-dimensional admissible sets, with 120
training, 40 validation and 200 held-out geometries from disjoint seed
streams. Learned constraint models are fitted on each evaluated instance from
3,000 labeled state-action samples, with 250 gradient steps for the nonlinear
model, and every arm receives 400 sampled proposals per instance. An arm
retains the required action when its displacement stays below
$0.02(\|u_{\rm req}\|+10^{-9})$. The context-free box's own exclusion of the
required action defines the 124/76 partition, and the approximate arms share
its inscribed-box bounds, which can contribute both feasibility and
conservatism.

The third study tests behavior beyond the training conditions. The original
held-out tests use the training reset ranges, and a prospectively declared
evaluation adds 42,000 episodes across the five tasks with stochastic physical
resets. It widens existing reset half-widths by $1.5\times$ and $2\times$ and
samples a $2\times$ outside-support shell, keeping selected policies and
governors fixed. PRY is not applicable because its physical reset is
deterministic. \suppsection{S14} and \suppref{tab:ood-ranges} give the
committed protocol, explicit reset interface and complete ranges. The SNAP
command and damage attribution reported in Results was conducted after this
evaluation and is labelled post hoc.

The fourth study perturbs the task models themselves. A sensitivity sweep
varies 21 constants across five multipliers from 0.5 to 2.0, with eight
scripted episodes per arm and case. It orders arms first by destructive
completions and, when both are zero, by safe completion, and its nine failed
cases are diagnosed after the sweep. Separately, a BAYONET experiment
preregisters a 150,000-step budget, SAC as the primary and PPO as a
replication algorithm, and a 50\% competence gate. Neither algorithm exceeds
10\% mean safe completion, and the gate and task were left unchanged after
this inconclusive result. \suppsection{S7} and \suppsection{S10} give
implementation checks, designs and full outcomes.

\subsection{Foundation-model commands}
\label{sec:vlm-method}
The foundation-model layer asks whether TRiX governs the commands that real
models produce. A frozen bank of 91 images covers 28 base configurations of
the PCB task: 21 appear in normal, rotated, occluded and misleading views, and
seven in one partial view each. Each provider received the same image and
prompt pairs with a 2,000-token output limit. The recorded aliases are
\texttt{claude-sonnet-4-5}, \texttt{gpt-4o} and \texttt{gemini-flash-latest};
they are not pinned backend snapshots \cite{ref36,ref37,ref38}. All raw
responses and parsed commands are retained, while backend IDs, per-call dates
and retry attribution are unavailable. Historical notes mention four retried
Gemini timeouts whose image IDs and successful variants cannot be recovered,
and the client also permits fallback outside those events.

The images make tilt visible by scaling it. Main-stratum display tilt is
approximately $20.669$ times the replayed mechanical curvature, and partial
views use $3.6$ times. Saved near-boundary mechanical tilts of
0.0358--0.042525~rad therefore appear as roughly $42.4^\circ$--$50.4^\circ$,
beyond the model's $0.075$-rad ($4.30^\circ$) fracture threshold if read
physically, so a displayed pose need not be a realizable intact-board state.
Positive scaling preserves ordering within the 84 main-stratum images but not
physical distance to a boundary, and all margin comparisons exclude the seven
partial views. Rendering reproducibility does not establish fidelity to
mechanical tilt. The stratum counts describe responses to this encoding, and
without an unscaled image control, effects of visual scaling cannot be
separated from differences between model runs.

A fixed parser maps each output to \texttt{LIFT}, \texttt{TILT\_X},
\texttt{TILT\_Y}, \texttt{PRESS}, \texttt{HOLD} or \texttt{ABORT}. Refusals
and ungroundable outputs are counted separately from grounded admissible and
inadmissible commands, and set membership alone defines inadmissibility, since
no independent semantic-correctness annotation is available. Each grounded
command is held for 40 steps in unfiltered and governed replay from the same
restored state and seed. A violation case contains at least one recorded
constraint violation, and prevention requires violations only in the
unfiltered arm. \suppsection{S11} gives the display formulas, trial ledger,
stratum counts and a conditional Gemini sensitivity bound.

\subsection{Physical platform}
\label{sec:hardware-methods}
The hardware layer places TRiX in the command path of a Seeed reBot B601-RS
arm with RobStride actuators, a 48-V supply and 1-Mbit/s CAN. Joints~1--5
receive 0.1-second MIT-style position/velocity commands, and Joint~6 runs an
approximately 200-Hz velocity controller. TRiX separates symbolic proposals,
decision receipts and approved executor targets, and protects Joint~1 with a
hard invariant. The final grasp implementation is identified by commit
\texttt{d296e6e} and tag \path{trix-hardware-soft-grasp-success-20260828}; its
calibrated sequence uses J3 gravity feed-forward and a disclosed Joint~2
positioning offset around engagement. In all six grasp-and-return trials, the
operator manually positions the object between the open jaws at the
calibrated grasp-ready pose before governed closure, lift and return. The
placement protocol for the three repetitions without video is
author-confirmed, and their logs record robot execution. Console logs,
passive CAN captures, source and configuration snapshots, video mappings and
checksums support the intervention experiment, the three video-backed trials
and the three logged repetitions. The platform has no independent
force/torque measurement and no autonomous perception-to-grasp stage
(\suppsection{S12}).

\subsection{Measurement, statistics and provenance}
\label{sec:statistics}
To measure what governance costs per command, the latency benchmark times the
complete $(o,a)\mapsto(a',\mathrm{info})$ call, including allocations,
per-call constraint construction and diagnostics. It uses 2,048 saved inputs
per task, five fresh processes and 256 warm-up calls per
implementation/process. Every arm sees the same per-task input sequence, with
task/arm order randomized in 64-input blocks. Durations are untrimmed integer
nanoseconds, cyclic garbage collection is disabled, and timer overhead is
recorded without subtraction. Each process uses CPU~0 and one native
numerical thread. OSQP reuses its problem and warm start, and its one-time
construction is reported separately. Policy/VLM inference, input generation,
simulation and robot communication fall outside the interval.
\suppsection{S8} records host and software versions, tolerances, raw arrays,
source hashes and the complete workload design.

The training seed, not the evaluation episode, is the experimental unit for
policy comparisons. We report ten-seed means together with every plotted seed
value. Selected contrasts use paired differences by seed identifier and exact
percentile bootstrap distributions over those ten differences, computed by
convolution; the intervals are descriptive, not multiplicity-adjusted tests.
Same-policy Stage~1 contrasts are kept distinct from separately trained
Stage~2 and cross-regime pairs. The preventive BATTERY and PCB cross-regime
contrasts also have recorded episode-seed mismatches, so they are paired only
at training-seed level (\suppref{tab:submitted-11}). The shifted reset study
uses the same estimator and training-seed pairing; its changes from historical
in-range estimates include episode-sampling variation because the schedules
differ, and within/outside-support strata are descriptive
(\suppsection{S14}). Procedural instances are the units of the geometry
study. VLM counts are descriptive over paired images and commands, views
clustered within 28 configurations are not independent physical replicates,
and no inferential cross-model test is reported.

Every experimental record is linked to the applicable environment, policy,
constraint, margin and outcome specifications, and a curated source map links
all main and supplementary figures and tables to data or executable source.
Correction notes retain affected historical files. Independent physical
calibration and unresolved backend identities remain provenance limits.
Figure~\ref{fig:seed-distributions} was regenerated from the retained
seed-level data with a supplied replacement plotting script; the original
plotting script remains unavailable.

\section{Results}

The Results answer four questions in sequence. Within the simulation
benchmark, we ask first whether the training regime changes a governor's
value, then what separates governors once the regime is fixed: the solver,
the task context, the specification or the representation of the constraint.
We then ask what happens when the benchmark moves beyond its training
conditions. Finally, we carry the same contract to commands from foundation
models, to a physical robot and to its runtime cost.

\subsection{The training regime decides whether a restriction suffices}
\label{sec:policy-results}
Circuit-board extraction shows most clearly that a governor and its policy act
as one system. Applied after SAC training, the componentwise
\texttt{box\_clip} restriction yields 0.0\% safe completion. With the same
restriction active during training, safe completion is 100.0\%
(Table~\ref{tab:policy-summary}), and all ten training seeds show the reversal
(Figure~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}a). The frozen policy is not the problem:
under TRiX it reaches 100.0\%, so the post-hoc failure lies in the
restriction. A policy trained under the box instead learns to work inside the
authority the box retains. The restriction is identical in both settings and
only the regime changes, which makes the regime a variable that any
evaluation of a governor must report.

\subsection{Task context, not the solver, separates governors}
\label{sec:context-results}
If the regime can reverse the verdict on a restriction, the next question is
what else does. Screw removal rules out the solver. Holding the constraint
fixed, \texttt{oracle\_tangent}, \texttt{qp\_matched} and TRiX each reach
88.7\% safe completion for SAC and 60.0\% for PPO under frozen-policy
evaluation, against 0.0\% without a governor, and the matched constrained
controls reach 100\% for both algorithms with filter-aware training. Given the
same admissible set, the methods agree exactly.

Task context, by contrast, separates governors even after training. On crank
removal, where the admissible forces turn with the part, the static
restriction stays at 0.0\% after filter-aware SAC training, while the
task-conditioned governor reaches 49.4\%, with seeds clustered near 0\% or
100\% (Figure~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}c). The two governors differ in both
angle-conditioned axial bounds and task-frame transverse representation, so
this pair measures their combined effect. Snap-fit release separates even more
sharply, because a restriction blind to the latch phase cannot tell a
premature pull from a permitted one. Within the training reset ranges, safe
completion is 5.2\% under the static restriction and 96.5\% under TRiX
(Figure~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}b). These are separately trained
policy-and-governor systems that also differ in latch-phase conditioning and
margin, so the gap measures the systems rather than the governor's causal
contribution alone. Under the shifted resets reported below, these rates fall
to 3.5\% and 59.9\%, respectively.

\subsection{Simple restrictions suffice when correctly specified}
\label{sec:sufficient-results}
The converse also holds: where a simple restriction already carries the
information a task needs, adaptation makes it sufficient. On prying,
filter-aware SAC reaches 88.5\% under the static restriction and 89.1\% under
the task-conditioned one, with closely aligned seed distributions and the same
zero-performing seed (Figure~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}d). On circuit-board
extraction in this regime, the box reaches 100.0\% and TRiX 99.8\%. These two
arms also differ in torque margin, a nominal 0.100~N\,m per-axis bound against
a robust coupled radius of approximately 0.087087~N\,m, so the pair measures
geometry and margin together rather than box-versus-disk geometry alone.

Battery peeling shows that the specification can matter more than the form of
the restriction. Earlier arms compared the 20-N flagged peel boundary with a
15-N preventive deformation threshold. Once componentwise and TRiX arms share
the same preventive target and margin construction, both reach 100\% safe
completion. An exact-arm audit corrected a historical aggregation that had
pooled similarly named BATTERY arms, setting Stage~1 SAC/TRiX safe completion
to 20.0\% with original records retained (\suppsection{S9.4}).
\suppsection{S9} reports the full policy tables and
\suppref{tab:submitted-11} the training-seed intervals and pairing exceptions.
On some tasks the nominal policy itself fails, leaving every frozen-policy arm
at zero, which limits the ranking those cells can support.

\subsection{Representation determines retained authority}
\label{sec:representation-results}
Context and specification enter a governor through how its constraints are
represented, which two studies examine directly. On 2,000 held-out
circuit-board proposals, componentwise clipping produces 2.9\% feasible
commands, a learned action-affine model 0.0\%, a learned nonlinear model
78.0\% after one correction and 92.3\% after iteration, and the analytical
oracle and exact projector 100\%. The affine result stays at 0.0\% as fitting
samples grow from 500 to 20,000, placing its failure in the representation
rather than the data. This single-seed study compares the implemented
representations, not learning in general or complete safety algorithms.

The procedural study widens the comparison to 200 admissible sets drawn from
box, ellipsoid, polytope, state-scaled, rotating and phase-gated families,
each with a required action inside its exact set. The context-free box
excludes that action in 124 instances and retains it in 76. Because the box's
own exclusion defines this partition, it is a constructed calibration rather
than an independent test of the box, and the informative results come from
the other arms (\suppref{tab:procedural-geometry}). Exact projection retains
the required action in 100\% of both groups, and the context-conditioned
oracle in 36.3\% and 100\%, respectively. The learned affine model retains it
in 0\% of both groups, including the group where a box succeeds. Every arm
returns feasible commands on 400 sampled proposals per instance, partly
because the approximate arms share the inscribed-box bounds, so feasibility
and retained authority prove to be distinct properties. Models are fitted
separately on each evaluated geometry, so the study measures representation
capacity rather than transfer of one learned model (\suppsection{S10}).

\subsection{Per-step admissibility ends at the training boundary}
\label{sec:ood-results}
Every result so far was obtained within the conditions the policies were
trained on. The prospectively declared shifted-reset evaluation asks what
happens beyond them, and it shows where per-step governance ends. In the
primary outside-support shell, snap-fit safe completion is 59.9\% under TRiX
and 3.5\% under static, down from the in-range 96.5\% and 5.2\%; as in range,
this compares complete policy-and-governor systems and does not isolate the
governor's causal contribution. Latch fractures, including timeouts, occur in
401 and 468 of 1,000 episodes per arm. Destructive completions, which combine
completed episodes with fracture or accumulated damage and exclude timeouts,
number 369 and 886 (\suppref{tab:snap-ood-attribution}). The advantage of TRiX
in safe completion is therefore far larger than its reduction in fractures.

A post-hoc replay audit locates the mechanism. Every TRiX command lies inside
the frozen executable bounds, yet all 401 TRiX fractures begin through
overdeflection in the softer-latch stratum, before any pull damage, while all
480 TRiX episodes in the harder-latch stratum complete safely. Admissible
commands, applied one step at a time, drove a softer latch into a damaging
state. These counts are observations within the sampled stiffness intervals,
not a safety bound over them; \suppsection{S14.4} reports the full partition,
the traces and how the executed margins relate to a later, tighter registry.

The other tasks shift less. Crank removal reaches 47.6\% under TRiX and
0.0\% under the static arm, with 22 destructive completions per 1,000 episodes
under TRiX, and matched screw arms remain equal at 87.9\% for SAC and 59.9\%
for PPO. Circuit-board extraction gives 0.0\%/99.6\% in Stage~1 and
100.0\%/99.8\% in Stage~2 for box/TRiX, and preventive battery arms give
100.0\%/100.0\%. Prying has a deterministic physical reset and is outside this
evaluation. \suppsection{S14}, \suppref{tab:ood-safe} and
\suppref{tab:ood-outcomes} report all three declared conditions, changes,
intervals and outcome categories, and the original in-range results are
unchanged.

\subsection{Governance of foundation-model commands}
\label{sec:vlm-results}
The simulation results concern policies trained on the benchmark. The second
foundation tests whether the same contract governs commands that no such
policy produced. Three recorded VLM runs received the same 91 circuit-board
images and prompt, giving 273 decisions. The labels Claude Sonnet, GPT-4o and
Gemini identify the configured aliases and recorded runs; resolved backend
versions are not recorded. The decisions partition into 16 refusals, 219
admissible commands and 38 inadmissible commands, with no ungroundable output
(Table~\ref{tab:vlm-results}), and each of the 257 grounded commands was
replayed with and without governance.

TRiX prevents all 31 observed constraint-violation cases and introduces none.
A case counts as prevented only when the unfiltered replay violates a
constraint and the governed replay from the same restored state and seed does
not, so changing a command earns no credit on its own. GPT-4o contributes nine
prevented cases and Gemini 22, while the three inadmissible Claude commands
caused no unfiltered violation. In one example, GPT-4o's response to the
\texttt{s06\_occluded} image requests \texttt{LIFT} at magnitude 0.8,
grounding to $(0,0,0.8)$: the unfiltered replay violates the constraint on
40/40 steps and the governed replay on 0/40. Every replay runs the full
40-step horizon without task completion or mechanical failure, so the
measured outcome is prevented constraint violation, not fractures or
successful extraction. The commands were elicited from visibility-scaled
images whose displayed tilts can exceed the model's intact-board range, and
the result measures governance of those recorded commands.

The models fail in different places. Thirty-five images elicit an
inadmissible proposal in at least one run and none in all three. Claude's
three inadmissible cases and GPT-4o's nine share no image, even when grouped
by base configuration, so no Gemini failure set could produce a three-way
overlap. A governor therefore cannot be tuned to one model's errors and
assumed to cover another's. The study measures one proposal per image across
repeated views of 28 configurations; it evaluates governance of proposals
rather than an autonomous agent loop or hallucination detection.

\subsection{Governance on a physical robot}
\label{sec:hardware-results}
The third foundation asks whether the contract changes what physical motors
actually do. On the B601-RS arm, TRiX sits in the command path. It rejects an
invalid symbolic transition and a proposed $+50^\circ$ motion of protected
Joint~1, producing no physical target, and it projects an excessive
$+50^\circ$ Joint~2 proposal to $+18^\circ$, so that only the corrected target
is transmitted and executed. The allowed return finishes with Joint~1 drift of
$+0.007^\circ$.

The frozen final implementation then completes three video-backed
grasp-and-return trials with the object retained
(Figure~\ref{fig:b601-hardware}), each with ten \texttt{ALLOW} decisions, for
30 governed decisions in total. The largest absolute Joint~1 return drift is
$0.027^\circ$ and the largest start-to-final residual over Joints~1--6 is
$0.122^\circ$. Three further logged repetitions complete normally and are
counted separately from the video-backed trials. In all six, the operator
places the object between the open jaws at a calibrated grasp pose, and the
governed sequence closes the gripper, lifts and returns. The arm carries no
independent wrench sensor: these trials establish the command boundary on
physical hardware, and force-level calibration of the six task models is the
next step (\suppsection{S12}).

\subsection{The cost of governance}
\label{sec:runtime-benchmark}
A governor that sits in every command path must also be cheap, and at this
boundary it costs microseconds per command. A benchmark of 225,280
complete-filter calls across 22 existing implementations, six tasks and five
fresh processes, on the recorded Intel Core Ultra 9 275HX host, gives matched
screw median/95th-percentile latencies of 1.255/1.556~$\mu$s for production
scalar TRiX, 12.578/15.534~$\mu$s for active-set QP and 14.924/19.895~$\mu$s
for reused OSQP. All matched arms pass the declared numerical agreement checks,
with a maximum OSQP action-coordinate difference of $1.013\times10^{-6}$.
Across the six registered TRiX arms, medians range from 1.123 to
11.770~$\mu$s. Implementation matters as much as formulation: the NumPy
analytical implementation takes 18.163~$\mu$s by median, slower than both QP
medians for the same geometry. \suppref{tab:runtime-latency} reports every
implementation and its process variation. All outliers are retained, and these
are measured costs on the recorded host rather than worst-case or end-to-end
robot latency.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
A runtime governor is usually presented as a component that can be judged on
its own. The central result of this work is that it cannot. The same
restriction on circuit-board extraction fails as a post-hoc filter and
succeeds as a training-time constraint, while on crank removal no amount of
training supplies the context a restriction omits. A governor's value is a
property of the system it joins, and evaluations should report post-hoc
governance and filter-aware learning separately, because each supports a
different claim.

What decides the outcome within that system is what the governor is told.
Given the same command set, analytical projection and a QP coincide on screw
removal, and given the same preventive specification, componentwise and
coupled arms coincide on battery peeling. The procedural study then separates
two properties that are easily conflated: a correction can be feasible and
still discard the action the task requires. The learned nonlinear
circuit-board correction shows how much an expressive learned model, iterated,
can recover, so the contribution here is an auditable execution contract and
an experimental map of when each kind of restriction suffices, rather than a
universal ranking of symbolic projection over learned or classical methods.
The timing results point the same way: the advantage belongs to the scalar
implementation, since the NumPy analytical version is slower than both matched
QP controls.

The experiments also show where per-step governance ends, and that boundary
defines what must be built next. Outside training support, every TRiX command
on snap-fit release stayed within bounds while fractures fell only from 468 to
401 per 1,000 episodes: admissible commands do not by themselves secure the
state they produce. In battery peeling, an internal short can keep heating
after force is removed, beyond the reach of any current-command filter. In
snap-fit release, suppressing a premature pull does not supply the latch
deflection the task needs. A correct projector also enforces whatever boundary
it is given, so the 20-N flagged battery limit allows deformation that begins
at 15~N. Probabilistic tightening can remove authority altogether: for the
crank axial family, $L/\sigma=5/1.235\simeq4.05$, whereas the declared
multiplier is approximately 4.488, a specification-feasibility failure
detailed in \suppsection{S13}. Each of these limits points to a specific
extension: state-aware constraints, sequence-level planning and calibrated
specifications.

These boundaries are empirical, and the study reports them in full. The
prescribed task-specific ordering holds in 96 of 105 perturbation cases; the
nine exceptions comprise six PCB boundary/model cases, one CRANK feasibility
failure and two BATTERY completion trade-offs with zero destructive
completions in both arms (\suppsection{S7}). A prospective BAYONET test is
inconclusive because neither policy algorithm reached its predeclared
competence gate, and it is reported as such.

Across its three foundations, the study carries one execution contract from
learned policies to foundation-model proposals to physical motors. The model
study shows governance intercepting real generative commands, on
visibility-scaled images of one task, and shows that different models fail on
different inputs. The hardware study shows rejection and projection changing
what physical actuators execute and supporting repeated manipulation, with a
calibrated grasp, manual object placement and a separate joint-space contract.
Neither calibrates the benchmark's force, torque or damage thresholds; that
requires independent physical measurements across contact uncertainty, object
variation and model mismatch. The natural next step is to join the three
layers: calibrated force and damage models, state estimation and sequence
planning, measured on hardware against the same contract. Execution
governance is most informative when its proposal distribution, task context
and physical specification are evaluated together, and TRiX makes each of them
explicit.
