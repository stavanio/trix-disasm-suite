\begin{abstract}
Robots dismantling electronic waste increasingly act on commands from
learned policies and vision-language models, yet plausible commands can break
the task's physical rules, such as pulling a screw that must turn.
Here we show that an execution governor, the layer deciding which commands
reach the motors, cannot be judged apart from the policy it serves, the task
information it uses, the correction it applies and the limits it enforces. We
study TRiX, which allows, projects or rejects each command. In six simulated
disassembly tasks, the same restriction gives 0\% safe completion when added
after training but 100\% during training, and changing only the
projection metric lowers completion from 60.0\% to 20.0\% while every forwarded
command stays admissible. Under shifted conditions, admissible commands still
fracture latches. TRiX prevents all 31 observed violations among 257 grounded commands from
Claude Sonnet, GPT-4o and Gemini, and on a robot arm cuts a 50$^\circ$ joint
command to 18$^\circ$ before motion.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} robotic disassembly; execution governance; action
projection; robot learning; vision-language models

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Electronic waste is among the fastest-growing waste streams, and recovering
components for reuse can require disassembly that preserves their integrity
\cite{ref1,ref2,ref3,ref4,ref5,ref6}. Automating that work is hard
because each operation hides a physical rule. A screw must turn as it
withdraws, or its thread strips; a latch must be deflected before a cover will
lift; a battery pressed too hard can short internally and keep heating after
the robot lets go. Whether a command is acceptable depends on these rules and
on where the task currently stands.

The commands increasingly come from learned policies and vision-language
models (VLMs), which propose manipulation actions from images, instructions
and experience \cite{ref8,ref9,ref10,ref11,ref12,ref13} and are taking on
reasoning and planning roles in collaborative robotics \cite{ref39}. Nothing
in these proposers guarantees that a command respects the physics of the part:
a model can name the right step and still command a pull where a turn was
needed. The failures at stake are hazards, such as a battery short, and losses
of asset integrity, such as a fractured circuit board, a stripped thread or a
broken latch. Both can be declared in advance as physical models. This paper
measures command admissibility and task outcomes under such models; physical
safety for a specific product further requires calibrating them against
measured forces.

Existing methods decide which proposals reach the motors at different points
in the system.
Constrained reinforcement learning shapes the policy under cost limits
\cite{ref16,ref17}, and Lagrangian methods adapt penalties as learning
proceeds \cite{ref18,ref19,ref20}. At execution, safety layers correct actions
through a learned constraint model \cite{ref21}, recovery policies take over
when estimated risk is high \cite{ref22}, and control barrier functions enforce
state-dependent safe sets under explicit dynamical and feasibility assumptions
\cite{ref23,ref24}, with extensions to higher relative degree \cite{ref40} and
time-varying constraints \cite{ref41}. Shields synthesized from temporal-logic
specifications correct a learner's actions \cite{ref42}, optimization layers
project network outputs onto constraint sets during training \cite{ref43}, and
predictive safety filters certify a proposed input over a horizon
\cite{ref44}. Table~\ref{tab:submitted-1} compares where representative
formulations intervene and what their guarantees assume.

Whichever method serves as the runtime filter, two factors outside its
mechanism decide what it achieves. The first is what the filter is told: which
constraints it represents, and which task information, such as the phase of
the task or the orientation of a part, it can use. The second is when the
policy meets it. A filter added to a trained policy must correct proposals
never shaped by it, whereas a policy trained with the filter in place, as
shields and optimization layers permit \cite{ref42,ref43}, can learn to work
inside it.

We study both factors with TRiX, an execution governor placed between the
proposer and the robot (Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-1}). For each proposed
command, TRiX selects the commands the task model admits from the task state
and a declared task context, and returns one of four decisions:
\texttt{ALLOW} passes an admissible command, \texttt{PROJECT} replaces an
inadmissible command with the nearest admissible one, \texttt{REJECT} refuses
a request that no correction can repair, and \texttt{INFEASIBLE} reports that
the specification leaves no usable command. Only allowed or projected commands
reach the executor. TRiX checks admissibility under the declared task model,
not semantic correctness. Because its admissible set follows the task's state and phase, we
call it a task-conditioned governor, in contrast to a static restriction that
applies fixed limits throughout a task; the range of commands a restriction
leaves available is its command authority.

We evaluate TRiX in three layers, each closer to deployment than the last. In
simulation, DISASM-Bench provides six analytical tasks, each stressing a
different kind of constraint: screw removal (SCREW), where axial and angular
motion are coupled; circuit-board extraction (PCB), with a coupled tilt-torque
limit; snap-fit release (SNAP), where a latch sets the order of operations;
crank removal (CRANK), whose force limits rotate with the part; battery
peeling (BATTERY), with a delayed internal hazard; and prying (PRY), whose
torque limit shrinks with insertion depth. Reinforcement-learning policies are
trained on each task with and without the governor. Commands proposed by
Claude Sonnet, GPT-4o and Gemini from shared images of the PCB task are
replayed with and without governance. On a Seeed reBot B601-RS
six-degree-of-freedom arm with a parallel gripper, TRiX sits in the command
path, so its decisions change what the motors execute.

Across these layers, a governor's value depends on factors outside the
governor. On PCB, the same componentwise restriction yields 0.0\%
safe completion when added after training and 100.0\% when present during
training. On SCREW, analytical projection and a QP produce identical outcomes
when given the same constraint, yet rescaling the correction metric changes
the outcomes of frozen policies while every forwarded command stays
admissible. On CRANK, the implemented task-conditioned system retains
authority that its static counterpart lacks even after training. Under shifted
SNAP conditions, every governed command stays within its bounds while latches
still fracture, so command admissibility does not imply safe evolution of the
task state. Replayed VLM commands and the physical arm then test the same
contract at two further interfaces. Execution governance is therefore a
property of the whole system, to be evaluated jointly with the proposer, the
task information and the specification rather than as an isolated filter.

\section{Methods}

The Methods first define the execution contract shared by every experiment,
then describe the three evaluation layers in the order the Results report
them: the simulation benchmark, the replayed VLM commands and the physical
arm.

\subsection{Execution contract}
\label{sec:authority}
\paragraph{Notation.} Let $x$ denote the observed task state and $c$ the
declared task context, such as the current latch phase in SNAP. A proposer
emits a command $\tilde u\in[-1,1]^m$ in normalized coordinates, each component
divided by a declared physical scale, and TRiX either forwards a command $u$ or
forwards nothing. The admissible set $\mathcal A(x,c)\subseteq[-1,1]^m$
contains the commands the task model allows in state $x$ under context $c$.
Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-1} uses the same symbols.

\paragraph{Decision rule.} TRiX maps $(x,c,\tilde u)$ to one of four decisions
and a forwarded command:
\begin{equation}
u=\begin{cases}
\tilde u, & \tilde u\in\mathcal A(x,c) \qquad (\texttt{ALLOW}),\\[2pt]
\Pi_{\mathcal A}(\tilde u), & \tilde u\notin\mathcal A(x,c),\ \text{a correction exists} \qquad (\texttt{PROJECT}),\\[2pt]
\varnothing, & \text{the request breaks a sequence or state rule} \qquad (\texttt{REJECT}),\\[2pt]
\varnothing, & \mathcal A(x,c)\ \text{leaves no usable command} \qquad (\texttt{INFEASIBLE}),
\end{cases}
\label{eq:contract}
\end{equation}
where $\varnothing$ means that nothing reaches the executor. \texttt{REJECT}
covers requests that no numerical correction can repair, such as a move between
task phases the task does not allow. When $\mathcal A(x,c)$ is a nonempty
closed convex set, the correction is the unique Euclidean projection
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathcal A}(\tilde u)=\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal A(x,c)}
\tfrac12\|u-\tilde u\|_2^2.
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
The normalization is itself part of the model: velocity, angular-velocity and
force errors share this metric only after each has a declared scale. Changing
the scales changes which admissible command is nearest, but not whether a
command is admissible: the membership guarantee is unchanged, while the
correction a proposer receives, and with it the task outcome of a frozen
policy, can change (Results). The scales are therefore part of the
specification; they are listed in \suppsection{S4} and were fixed in the task
code before any policy was trained.

\paragraph{Coupled motion.} Screw removal shows why the representation of
$\mathcal A$ matters before any learning enters. A screw of pitch $p$ advances
one pitch per turn, so ideal motion couples axial velocity $v_z$ to angular
velocity $\omega_z$:
\begin{equation}
v_z=k\,\omega_z,\qquad k=\frac{p}{2\pi}.
\label{eq:pitch}
\end{equation}
With command scales $S_v$ and $S_\omega$, the normalized components are
$a_v=v_z/S_v$ and $a_\omega=\omega_z/S_\omega$, the axes of
Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-2}. A proposal is then $\tilde u=(\tilde a_v,\tilde
a_\omega)$, the ideal line is $a_v=\alpha\,a_\omega$ with coupling coefficient
$\alpha=kS_\omega/S_v$, and projection onto it is
\begin{equation}
\Pi_0(\tilde u)=
\frac{\alpha\tilde a_v+\tilde a_\omega}{1+\alpha^2}
\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:helix}
\end{equation}
No pair of independent bounds on $a_v$ and $a_\omega$ can express this
coupling. The implemented SCREW task widens the line into a finite-tolerance
strip, bounded by command and radial-force limits, that occupies 2.0\% of the
normalized command slice in Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-2}a. Clipping a proposal
to a componentwise box can leave it outside that strip, whereas projection
lands it on the strip (Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-2}b); \suppsection{S2} gives the
projection operator or restriction used for each task. A suitably specified
linear-constraint quadratic program (QP) represents the same set, so the
experiments can compare solution mechanisms with the specification held
fixed.

\paragraph{Representation properties.} Four properties of command sets frame
the experiments; the Supplementary Information gives the assumptions and proofs
(\suppsection{S3}).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Clipping is projection onto a box.} If $\mathcal A=\prod_i[\ell_i,h_i]$, then
\begin{equation}
[\Pi_{\mathcal A}(\tilde u)]_i=\min\{\max\{\tilde u_i,\ell_i\},h_i\}.
\label{eq:clip}
\end{equation}
\item \emph{A phase-independent set gives up phase authority.} A set valid in
phases $c_1$ and $c_2$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\mathcal A_{\rm static}\subseteq\mathcal A(x,c_1)\cap\mathcal A(x,c_2),
\label{eq:phase}
\end{equation}
and so discards any authority that only one phase allows.
\item \emph{An orientation-independent set gives up rotating authority.} Over
all rotations $R_\theta$ of a rectangle with half-widths $a\geq b>0$,
\begin{equation}
\bigcap_{\theta}R_\theta\bigl([-a,a]\times[-b,b]\bigr)=\{u:\|u\|_2\leq b\},
\label{eq:rotation}
\end{equation}
so a fixed square $[-h,h]^2$ valid at every orientation requires
$h\leq b/\sqrt2$.
\item \emph{Tightening can remove all authority.} A Gaussian-tightened interval
\begin{equation}
|y|\leq L-z\sigma
\label{eq:tighten}
\end{equation}
keeps nonzero authority exactly when $z\sigma<L$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope of the contract.} TRiX takes as given an observed state, the
task context needed to select the applicable constraint, declared command
scales and an admissible-set specification. It does not discover the
specification; it enforces whatever set it is given. It guarantees that a
forwarded command satisfies the implemented constraint whenever the projection
or correction is well defined, which for Eq.~\eqref{eq:projection} requires a
nonempty closed convex set. Closed-loop stability and forward invariance for an
unmodeled plant, task completion and physical safety for an uncalibrated
product require dynamical analysis and calibration beyond this contract. How a
trained policy adapts to a restriction is measured empirically rather than
stated as a proposition (\suppsection{S3}).

\subsection{Simulation benchmark}
\label{sec:design}
DISASM-Bench turns six common disassembly operations into analytical models.
Each task pairs a command interface with the constraint that makes the task
hard and the damage that follows when that constraint is broken
(Table~\ref{tab:submitted-2}).

SCREW and PCB the constraint couples command components, so a componentwise
box misrepresents it. In SNAP and CRANK the admissible set changes with the
latch phase or the crank angle, so a fixed set must either discard authority or
admit commands the current phase forbids. In BATTERY the damage the specification guards against
begins below the flagged boundary, and in PRY the torque limit shrinks as the

restrictions described below. The force, torque, damage and thermal thresholds
are model specifications, not measured hardware limits.

\subsubsection{Task models and outcomes}
\label{sec:tasks}
Each task maps three normalized command coordinates ($m=3$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:contract}) to its own physical interface. SCREW commands axial
and angular velocity $v_z^c,\omega_z^c$ and radial force $F_r$; PCB commands
tilt torques $\tau_x,\tau_y$ and lift force $F_z$; SNAP commands
latch-deflection, pull and lateral forces $F_{\rm def},F_{\rm pull},F_{\rm
lat}$; CRANK commands forces $F_x,F_y,F_z$; BATTERY commands peel force $F_{\rm
peel}$ and lateral forces $F_{\ell1},F_{\ell2}$; and PRY, which levers a bonded
part open, commands tool torque $\tau$, insertion force $F_{\rm ins}$ and
lateral force $F_{\rm lat}$, with a torque limit that depends on insertion
depth $d$.

A versioned registry binds each task model, SCREW v3 and the five v2 tasks of
Table~\ref{tab:submitted-2}, to its environment and named filters. Every
observation has ten float32 entries whose meanings are task-specific
(\suppref{tab:submitted-7}), with
unused entries padded with zeros; PCB, for example, observes lift and lift
velocity, two tilt angles and their rates, accumulated damage and a fracture
flag. Commands are mapped from $[-1,1]^3$ through the physical scales in
\suppref{tab:action-scales}. Each outer action advances $1/240$~s of simulated time,
integrated with 40 internal substeps for SCREW, ten for PCB, SNAP, CRANK and
BATTERY, and directly for PRY. Progress and termination are task-specific.

Every episode ends in one outcome defined by a single contract. Safe
completion, the primary outcome throughout, requires successful task
termination without the task's declared outcome-affecting violation. The
contract separates it from completion with such a violation, from destructive
completion and from the remaining unsuccessful outcomes, and it records
diagnostic events separately (\suppref{tab:submitted-8}). Reset parameters, their distributions and
ranges, and whether each is used for training or evaluation are tabulated in
\suppref{tab:submitted-9} and detailed in \suppsection{S4.6}; risk
allocations, noise and reset variation all come from the frozen task code.

The analytical environments generate every reported dynamic and outcome.
Fig.~\ref{fig:disasm-workspaces} renders their recorded states in PyBullet
\cite{ref33} from a per-panel state manifest, for visualization only: the
illustrated gripper shows the command interface, while arm and contact
dynamics lie outside the task models. An earlier PyBullet prototype imposed the
screw pitch through a gear constraint and therefore could not validate that
pitch independently; we withdrew the associated validation argument.

\subsubsection{Policies, regimes and comparators}
\label{sec:training-methods}
PPO and SAC are trained with Stable-Baselines3 2.9.0 \cite{ref45}, Gymnasium
1.3.0 and two-layer $[256,256]$ networks on CPU, using ten independent training
seeds, 0--9, for every comparison. Two regimes mirror the two ways a governor
can meet a policy. In Stage~1, a policy is trained without any restriction, a
checkpoint is selected, and each runtime arm is applied to that same frozen
policy; we call this post-hoc governance. In Stage~2, a separate policy is
trained with its restriction inside the environment transition, while replay
and rollout buffers retain the nominal policy action; we call this
filter-aware training. The training adapter separates termination from
time-limit truncation and restores NumPy random state after interleaved
evaluation.

A runtime arm is any restriction placed between policy and environment, and
every arm, TRiX included, is a governor in this sense.
Table~\ref{tab:submitted-3} lists the arms and the role each plays.

The arms differ in what they know about the task. The unfiltered reference
\texttt{none} knows nothing. \texttt{box\_clip} bounds each command component
independently and so ignores coupling; \texttt{static\_clip} applies the task's
bounds without its phase or orientation; \texttt{oracle\_tangent} corrects along
analytical local gradients; \texttt{qp\_matched} solves a QP over the exact
SCREW constraint; and TRiX projects onto the task-conditioned set. Any arm may
tighten its limits by a margin that absorbs command noise.

Each comparison changes one factor where possible and states what it changes
where not. Two controls hold the specification fixed. For SCREW, the matched QP
and the analytical solver minimize the same objective,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:projection}, over the same finite helical slab, action bounds and
radial limit, so the comparison isolates the solution mechanism; state-space
barrier synthesis is a separate construction. For BATTERY, the preventive arms
share the 15-N threshold and margin construction. The other pairs vary more
than one factor and are reported that way: the PCB and SNAP arms retain their
implemented margin differences, and CRANK combines frame and angle
conditioning. The policy matrix contains only PPO and SAC task policies. It
includes neither a PPO-Lagrangian policy, which adds a learned cost penalty to
PPO, nor a policy trained with the learned safety layer of ref.~\cite{ref21}
(SafeLayer); learned constraint representations are studied separately below,
and the literature methods in Table~\ref{tab:submitted-1} keep their original
assumptions and are positioned conceptually rather than run as baselines.

A predeclared rule selects checkpoints so that no arm benefits from tuning. At
each task budget, checkpoints saved every 1,000 steps are screened on 30 fixed
validation episodes. Three-checkpoint windows are ranked by mean safe
completion, and the centers of the three best windows receive 70 additional
validation episodes. Selection is lexicographic: highest safe completion, then
lowest destructive completion, highest normalized progress and lowest
episode-level violation incidence, with the earliest checkpoint breaking any
remaining tie. Each selected policy is tested on 100 held-out episodes disjoint
from validation, and pilot seeds are excluded. Historical BATTERY prefix
pooling and the PCB oracle episode-seed correction are documented alongside
preserved originals. For SCREW training seed 0, the oracle arm was evaluated on
a different block of held-out episodes from the other arms; TRiX and the
matched QP share every episode for every seed; the full policy tables use exact-arm counts, and no
checkpoint was reselected during that audit. \suppref{tab:submitted-10} gives the frozen training and selection configuration;
software fingerprints, checkpoint-selection records and episode-seed
exceptions are given in \suppsection{S5} and \suppsection{S6}.

\subsubsection{Representation, shift and sensitivity studies}
\label{sec:model-validation}
Five further studies stress the benchmark beyond the main policy comparisons.
Two examine constraint representation apart from trained policies. The PCB
decomposition uses the archived seed-0 run, fitting learned models on 20,000
samples and testing them on 2,000 held-out proposals, together with an
affine-model sweep over 500--20,000 fitting samples. The procedural benchmark
generates six families of three-dimensional admissible sets, with 120
training, 40 validation and 200 held-out geometries from disjoint seed streams.
Learned constraint models are fitted on each evaluated instance from 3,000
labeled state-action samples, with 250 gradient steps for the nonlinear model,
and every arm receives 400 sampled proposals per instance. An arm retains the
required action $u_{\rm req}$ when its displacement stays below
$0.02(\|u_{\rm req}\|+10^{-9})$. The context-free box's own exclusion of the
required action defines the 124/76 partition, and the approximate arms share
its inscribed-box bounds, which can contribute both feasibility and
conservatism.

The third study tests behavior beyond the training conditions. The original
held-out tests use the training reset ranges, and a prospectively declared
evaluation adds 42,000 episodes across the five tasks with stochastic physical
resets. It widens each randomized reset range about its center to $1.5\times$
and $2\times$ its half-width and adds a $2\times$ outside-support shell that
samples resets only from outside the training support, keeping selected
policies and governors fixed. PRY is not applicable because its physical reset
is deterministic. \suppsection{S14} and \suppref{tab:ood-ranges} give the
committed protocol, explicit reset interface and complete ranges. The SNAP
command and damage attribution reported in the Results was conducted after this
evaluation and is labeled post hoc.

The fourth study perturbs the task models themselves. A sensitivity sweep
varies 21 constants across five multipliers from 0.5 to 2.0, with eight
scripted episodes per arm and case. It orders arms first by destructive
completions and, when both are zero, by safe completion, and its nine failed
cases are diagnosed after the sweep. Separately, a bayonet-mount release task
(BAYONET), preregistered as a prospective test, fixes a 15,000-step training budget per seed,
SAC as the primary and PPO as a replication algorithm, and a 50\% competence
gate, the minimum safe completion required before its governors are compared.
Neither algorithm exceeds 10\% mean safe completion, and the gate and task were
left unchanged after this inconclusive result. \suppsection{S7} and
\suppsection{S10} give implementation checks, designs and full outcomes.

The fifth study varies the correction metric with the admissible set fixed.
The selected Stage~1 SCREW policies are re-evaluated without retraining under
TRiX projection with the axial command scale $S_v$ multiplied by
a factor $\kappa$ of 0.5, 1 or 2 in the projection metric, on each policy's archived
100 held-out episodes. The run is valid only if $\kappa=1$ reproduces every
archived TRiX safe-completion count, which it does for all 20 policies
(\suppsection{S9.5}).

\subsection{Vision-language model command replay}
\label{sec:vlm-method}
A frozen bank of 91 images covers 28 base configurations of the PCB task: 21
appear in normal, rotated, occluded and misleading views, and seven in one
partial view each. Three recorded model runs received the same image and prompt
pairs with a 2,000-token output limit, giving 273 decisions. The recorded
aliases are \texttt{claude-sonnet-4-5}, \texttt{gpt-4o} and
\texttt{gemini-flash-latest}; the labels Claude Sonnet, GPT-4o and Gemini refer
to these aliases and runs, which are not pinned backend snapshots
\cite{ref36,ref37,ref38}. All raw responses and parsed commands are retained,
while backend identifiers, per-call dates and retry attribution are
unavailable. Historical notes mention four retried Gemini timeouts whose image
identifiers and successful variants cannot be recovered, and the client also
permits fallback outside those events.

The images make tilt visible by scaling it. In the 84 main-stratum images, the
21 configurations shown in four views, display tilt is approximately $20.669$
times the replayed mechanical curvature, and partial views use $3.6$ times.
Mechanical tilts saved for states near the fracture boundary,
0.0358--0.042525~rad, therefore appear as roughly $42.4^\circ$--$50.4^\circ$,
beyond the model's $0.075$-rad ($4.30^\circ$) fracture threshold if read
physically, so a displayed pose need not be a realizable intact-board state.
Positive scaling preserves ordering within the 84 main-stratum images but not
physical distance to a boundary, and all margin comparisons exclude the seven
partial views. Rendering reproducibility does not establish fidelity to
mechanical tilt. Without an unscaled image control, effects of visual scaling
cannot be separated from differences between model runs.

A fixed parser maps each response to one of six motion primitives,
\texttt{LIFT}, \texttt{TILT\_X}, \texttt{TILT\_Y}, \texttt{PRESS},
\texttt{HOLD} or \texttt{ABORT}, scaled to a normalized command. A response the
parser can map is grounded, and one it cannot is ungroundable. Refusals and
ungroundable outputs are counted separately from grounded admissible and
inadmissible commands, and set membership alone defines inadmissibility, since
no independent semantic-correctness annotation is available. Each grounded
command is held for 40 steps in unfiltered and governed replay from the same
restored state and seed. A violation case contains at least one recorded
constraint violation, and prevention requires violations in the unfiltered
replay only, so changing a command earns no credit on its own.
\suppsection{S11} gives the display formulas, trial ledger, stratum counts and
a conditional Gemini sensitivity bound.

\subsection{Hardware verification}
\label{sec:hardware-methods}
The hardware layer places TRiX in the command path of a Seeed reBot B601-RS, a
six-degree-of-freedom arm with a parallel gripper, RobStride actuators, a 48-V
supply and a 1-Mbit/s CAN bus. Joints~1--5 receive position and velocity
commands every 0.1 seconds in the actuators' MIT control mode, and Joint~6 runs
an approximately 200-Hz velocity controller. TRiX keeps proposed task-level
actions, logged decisions and approved joint targets separate, and protects
Joint~1 with a hard invariant that rejects proposals to move it. The calibrated
sequence uses gravity feed-forward on Joint~3 and a disclosed Joint~2
positioning offset around engagement. In all six grasp-and-return trials, the
operator manually positions the object between the open jaws at the calibrated
grasp-ready pose before governed closure, lift and return. The placement
protocol for the three repetitions without video is author-confirmed, and their
logs record robot execution. Console logs, passive CAN captures, source and
configuration snapshots, video mappings and checksums support the intervention
experiment, the three video-backed trials and the three logged repetitions.
The governor, trial scripts and tests used on the arm are included unchanged in
the code repository (Code Availability). The platform has no independent force
or torque measurement and no autonomous perception-to-grasp stage
(\suppsection{S12}).

\subsection{Latency measurement and statistical analyses}
\label{sec:statistics}
The latency benchmark times the complete governor call
$(x,\tilde u)\mapsto(u,\mathrm{info})$, from observation and proposal to
approved command and diagnostics, including allocations and per-call
constraint construction. It times 225,280 calls to 22 complete governor
implementations across six tasks and five fresh processes on an Intel Core
Ultra 9 275HX host, with 2,048 saved inputs per task and 256 warm-up calls per
implementation and process. Every arm sees the same per-task input sequence,
with task and arm order randomized in 64-input blocks. Durations are untrimmed
integer nanoseconds, cyclic garbage collection is disabled, and timer overhead
is recorded without subtraction. Each process uses CPU~0 and one native
numerical thread. OSQP \cite{ref46} reuses its problem and warm start, and its
one-time construction is reported separately. Policy and VLM inference, input
generation, simulation and robot communication fall outside the interval.
\suppsection{S8} records host and software versions, tolerances, raw arrays,
source hashes and the complete workload design.

The training seed, not the evaluation episode, is the experimental unit for
policy comparisons. We report ten-seed means together with every plotted seed
value. Selected contrasts use paired differences by seed identifier and exact
percentile bootstrap distributions over those ten differences, computed by
convolution; the intervals are descriptive, not multiplicity-adjusted tests.
Same-policy Stage~1 contrasts are kept distinct from separately trained
Stage~2 and cross-regime pairs. The preventive BATTERY and PCB cross-regime
contrasts also have recorded episode-seed mismatches, so they are paired only
at training-seed level (\suppref{tab:submitted-11}). The shifted-reset study
uses the same estimator and training-seed pairing; its changes from historical
in-range estimates include episode-sampling variation because the schedules
differ, and the within-support and outside-support strata are descriptive
(\suppsection{S14}). Procedural instances are the units of the geometry study.
VLM counts are descriptive over paired images and commands, views clustered
within 28 configurations are not independent physical replicates, and no
inferential cross-model test is reported.

\section{Results}

The Results follow the three evaluation layers and close with the latency the
governor adds to the command path. Safe completion, finishing a task without
its declared outcome-affecting violation, is the primary outcome in simulation.

\subsection{Simulation}
\label{sec:sim-results}
In simulation, four factors outside the governor's correction rule decide what
it achieves: when the policy meets it, which admissible command it substitutes,
what task information it encodes and how it represents the constraint. A fifth
result separates admissible commands from a safe task state. Each is shown on
the task whose constraint exposes it most directly.
Table~\ref{tab:policy-summary} collects the key comparisons within the training
reset ranges, and Fig.~\ref{fig:seed-distributions} shows the seed-level
distributions behind four of them. \suppref{tab:submitted-4} and
\suppref{tab:stage2-key} give the full Stage~1 and Stage~2 results.


\subsubsection{Training regime}
\label{sec:policy-results}
The same restriction fails or succeeds depending only on when the policy meets
it. PCB isolates this because its constraint couples the two tilt torques,
which a componentwise box cannot represent. Applied after SAC training, the box
restriction \texttt{box\_clip} yields 0.0\% safe completion; present during
training, the same restriction yields 100.0\%, and the reversal holds in all
ten training seeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}a). The frozen policy
reaches 100.0\% under TRiX, so the post-hoc failure lies in the restriction
applied to that policy, not in the policy. Trained under the box, the policy
learns to work inside the authority the box retains. The training regime is
therefore an experimental variable that every evaluation of a governor must
report.

\subsubsection{Solver, metric and task context}
\label{sec:context-results}
With the admissible set and correction metric held fixed, the three tested
solvers produce identical task outcomes. Under post-hoc governance on SCREW, the analytical reference
\texttt{oracle\_tangent}, the matched QP \texttt{qp\_matched} and TRiX each
reach 88.7\% safe completion for SAC and 60.0\% for PPO, against 0.0\% without
a governor. With filter-aware training, the matched constrained controls reach
100\% for both algorithms.

The correction metric, by contrast, changes outcomes. Projection returns the
nearest admissible command in normalized coordinates, so rescaling a command
scale changes which admissible command replaces a proposal while every
forwarded command stays admissible. Re-evaluated on their archived held-out
episodes with the axial scale halved or doubled, the same frozen policies fall
from 88.7\% to 67.7\% and 76.2\% safe completion for SAC, and from 60.0\% to
50.0\% and 20.0\% for PPO (Methods; \suppsection{S9.5}). No episode at any scale ends in
destructive completion or mechanical failure: the lost episodes stall until
timeout or, for SAC at the doubled scale, complete with a recorded violation.
The scales were declared in the task code before any policy was trained. A
frozen policy therefore depends on which admissible command a governor
substitutes, not only on which commands it admits.

Task context separates the implemented systems even after filter-aware
training. On CRANK, where the admissible forces turn with the part, the static
system stays at 0.0\% safe completion and the task-conditioned system reaches
49.4\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}c). That mean is a mixture of seeds
near 0\% and near 100\%: task-conditioned training sometimes finds a completing
strategy and sometimes fails entirely. On SNAP, a restriction blind to the
latch phase cannot distinguish a premature pull from a permitted one, and safe
completion is 5.2\% under the static restriction and 96.5\% under TRiX
(Fig.~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}b). Both pairs also differ in a second
factor, CRANK in transverse frame representation and SNAP in margin (Methods),
so each compares complete policy-and-governor systems.

\subsubsection{Sufficiency of simple restrictions}
\label{sec:sufficient-results}
Simple restrictions suffice when they already carry what the task needs. On
PRY, filter-aware SAC reaches 88.5\% safe completion under the static
restriction and 89.1\% under TRiX, with closely aligned seed distributions and
the same zero-performing seed (Fig.~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}d). On PCB in
this regime, the box reaches 100.0\% and TRiX 99.8\%; these arms differ in both
geometry and margin, a nominal 0.100~N\,m per-axis bound against a coupled
limit tightened for command noise to approximately 0.087087~N\,m.

On BATTERY, the specification decides the outcome. The original arms compared
the 20-N flagged peel boundary with a 15-N preventive deformation threshold;
once the componentwise and TRiX arms share the same preventive target and
margin construction, both reach 100\% safe completion. An exact-arm audit
corrected a historical aggregation that had pooled similarly named BATTERY
arms, which sets Stage~1 SAC safe completion under TRiX to 20.0\%; the original
records are retained (\suppsection{S9.4}). Where the nominal policy itself
fails, every frozen-policy arm sits at zero and those cells cannot rank
governors. \suppsection{S9} reports the full policy tables and
\suppref{tab:submitted-11} the training-seed intervals and pairing exceptions.

\subsubsection{Constraint representation}
\label{sec:representation-results}
How a governor represents a constraint decides whether its corrections land
inside it. On 2,000 held-out PCB proposals, the share of feasible corrected
commands is 2.9\% for componentwise clipping and 0.0\% for a learned model
linear in the action (action-affine), as in safety layers \cite{ref21}. A
learned nonlinear model reaches 78.0\% after one correction and 92.3\% after
iteration, and the analytical oracle and exact projector reach 100\%. The
affine model stays at 0.0\% as fitting samples grow from 500 to 20,000, which
places its failure in the representation rather than the data. This
comparison uses one archived training seed.

Feasibility and retained authority are different properties. Across 200
admissible sets drawn from box, ellipsoid, polytope, state-scaled, rotating and
phase-gated families, a single box that ignores task context, the context-free
box, excludes the required action in 124 instances and retains it in 76, a
split its own exclusion defines (\suppref{tab:procedural-geometry}). Exact
projection retains the required action in 100\% of both groups. A
context-conditioned oracle, limited to an inscribed box inside each set, retains
it in 36.3\% and 100\%, respectively, and the learned affine model in 0\% of
both groups. Yet every arm returns feasible commands on 400 sampled proposals
per instance, partly because the approximate arms share that inscribed box: a
correction can satisfy the constraint and still discard the action the task
needs. Models are fitted on each evaluated geometry, so the study measures
representation capacity rather than transfer of one learned model
(\suppsection{S10}).

\subsubsection{Distribution shift}
\label{sec:ood-results}
Admissible commands do not guarantee a safe task state. The prospectively
declared shifted-reset evaluation samples resets beyond the training ranges,
within the same analytical task models. In the primary outside-support shell,
SNAP safe completion falls from 96.5\% to 59.9\% under TRiX and from 5.2\% to
3.5\% under the static restriction. Latch fractures, including timeouts, occur
in 401 and 468 of 1,000 episodes per arm, and destructive completions,
completed episodes with fracture or accumulated damage excluding timeouts,
number 369 and 886 (\suppref{tab:snap-ood-attribution}). TRiX keeps a large
advantage in safe completion and a small one in fractures.

A post-hoc replay audit identifies the mechanism. Every TRiX command lies
inside the bounds the governor enforced, yet all 401 TRiX fractures begin
through overdeflection in episodes with softer latches, before any pull damage,
while all 480 TRiX episodes with harder latches complete safely. Admissible
commands, applied one step at a time, drove softer latches into damaging
states. These counts describe the sampled stiffness intervals;
\suppsection{S14.4} reports the full partition, the traces and a comparison
with a later, tighter margin specification.

The other tasks shift less. CRANK reaches 47.6\% under TRiX and 0.0\% under the
static restriction, with 22 destructive completions per 1,000 TRiX episodes,
and the matched SCREW arms remain equal at 87.9\% for SAC and 59.9\% for PPO.
PCB gives 0.0\% for the box and 99.6\% for TRiX in Stage~1, and 100.0\% and
99.8\% in Stage~2; the preventive BATTERY arms both give 100.0\%. PRY has a
deterministic physical reset and is outside this evaluation.
\suppsection{S14}, \suppref{tab:ood-safe} and \suppref{tab:ood-outcomes} report
all three declared conditions, changes, intervals and outcome categories; the
original in-range results are unchanged.

\subsection{Hardware verification}
\label{sec:hardware-results}
On the B601-RS, TRiX decisions change what the motors execute. TRiX sits
between task-level proposals and the joint targets sent to the actuators
(Methods). In the intervention experiment, it rejects a request to move
between task phases in an order the task does not allow, and a proposed
$+50^\circ$ motion of Joint~1, which the contract protects; neither produces a
physical target. It projects an excessive $+50^\circ$ Joint~2 proposal to
$+18^\circ$, and only the corrected target is transmitted and executed. The
allowed return that follows ends with Joint~1 $+0.007^\circ$ from its starting
angle.

The frozen final implementation then completes three video-backed
grasp-and-return trials with the object retained, each with ten \texttt{ALLOW}
decisions, for 30 governed decisions in total. Fig.~\ref{fig:b601-hardware}
follows one trial from approach to return, and \suppref{tab:b601-hardware} lists
all three. Across these trials, Joint~1
returns to within $0.027^\circ$ of its starting angle, and no joint among
Joints~1--6 ends more than $0.122^\circ$ from where it began. Three further
logged repetitions complete normally and are counted separately. In all six,
the operator places the object between the open jaws at a calibrated grasp
pose, and the governed sequence closes the gripper, lifts and returns. The arm
carries no independent wrench sensor, so these trials verify the command
boundary on physical hardware, while the force levels of the task models
remain model specifications (\suppsection{S12}).

\subsection{Latency in the command path}
\label{sec:runtime-benchmark}
A governor placed in the command path must return its decision well within
the interval between actuator commands, which on the B601-RS is 0.1 seconds
for Joints~1--5. TRiX takes microseconds. On the shared SCREW constraint,
median and 95th-percentile latencies are 1.255 and 1.556~$\mu$s for the
production TRiX implementation, 12.578 and 15.534~$\mu$s for an active-set QP
solver, and 14.924 and 19.895~$\mu$s for the OSQP solver reusing its setup
between calls. The three return the same commands to within
$1.013\times10^{-6}$ in any normalized coordinate and pass the declared
agreement checks. Across the TRiX implementations for the six tasks, median
latency ranges from 1.123 to 11.770~$\mu$s.

Implementation matters as much as formulation: a NumPy analytical
implementation of the same geometry takes 18.163~$\mu$s by median, slower than
both QP solvers. \suppref{tab:runtime-latency} reports every implementation and
its variation across processes. All outliers are retained, and these are
measured costs on the recorded host (Methods), not worst-case or end-to-end
robot latency.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
A runtime governor's value is a property of the policy-governor system, not of
the governor. The experiments show this on three axes. When the policy meets
the governor: on PCB, the same restriction yields 0.0\% safe completion as a
post-hoc filter and 100.0\% as a training-time constraint. Which admissible
command it substitutes: on SCREW, rescaling the correction metric moves
frozen-policy safe completion from 88.7\% to as low as 67.7\% for SAC and from
60.0\% to 20.0\% for PPO, while every forwarded command remains admissible.
What task information it encodes: on CRANK and SNAP, the implemented static
systems, which lack the rotating frame or the latch phase, lose authority that
training does not recover, although each pair also differs in a second
implementation choice. With the specification and metric held fixed, the
tested solvers coincide: analytical projection and a QP give identical outcomes
on SCREW.

These results change how governors should be evaluated. Policies adapt to
interventions during training, and shields and optimization layers already act
while a policy learns \cite{ref42,ref43}; the consequence shown here is that
post-hoc and filter-aware evaluations answer different questions, and a
governor validated in one setting carries no guarantee into the other. For
frozen proposers, including foundation models whose weights are not retrained
around a filter, the correction rule belongs to the safety specification and
needs the same declaration and testing as the admissible set. Representation
decides how much authority a correction retains: the procedural study shows a
correction can be feasible and still discard the action the task requires.
Learned representations remain viable where they capture the constraint, as
the iterated nonlinear model does on PCB (Results, ``Constraint
representation'').

Per-step admissibility is also not a guarantee about the task state. Outside
training support, every TRiX command on SNAP stayed within bounds while
fractures fell only from 468 to 401 per 1,000 episodes. In BATTERY, an internal
short can keep heating after force is removed, beyond the reach of any filter
on current commands, and a correct projector enforcing the 20-N flagged limit
still allows deformation that begins at 15~N. Probabilistic tightening can
remove authority altogether: tightening a limit $L$ by $z$ noise standard
deviations $\sigma$ leaves usable commands only while $z\sigma<L$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:tighten}), and for the CRANK axial limit
$L/\sigma=5/1.235\simeq4.05$, below the declared multiplier of approximately
4.488, so the tightened limit admits no command (\suppsection{S13}). These
boundaries point to state-aware constraints of the kind control barrier
functions provide \cite{ref23,ref40}, sequence-level certification as in
predictive safety filters \cite{ref44}, and calibrated specifications.

In the scripted parameter-sensitivity study, which perturbs 21 model
constants, the predicted ordering of governors persists in 96 of 105 tested
cases; the nine exceptions comprise six PCB boundary or model cases, one CRANK
feasibility failure and two BATTERY completion trade-offs with zero destructive
completions in both arms (\suppsection{S7}). A preregistered bayonet-mount task
is inconclusive because neither policy algorithm reached its predeclared
competence gate, and it is reported as such. The policy matrix trains PPO and
SAC; the state-aware and predictive methods of Table~\ref{tab:submitted-1} are
positioned rather than trained, and the matched QP shows that TRiX gains
nothing from its solver once the specification is fixed.

Beyond simulation, the same contract governs recorded commands from three
foundation models and the joint targets of a physical arm, on visibility-scaled
images of one task and with manual object placement. Calibrating the
benchmark's force, torque and damage thresholds against physical measurements,
across contact uncertainty, object variation and model mismatch, would turn
those thresholds from model specifications into measured limits. Governance at this boundary costs microseconds; the speed comes
from the scalar implementation, since the NumPy analytical version is slower
than both matched QP controls.

\section{Conclusions}
Execution governance decides which proposed commands reach a robot, and its
value is a property of the system it joins. Across six simulated disassembly
tasks, replayed commands from three VLMs and a six-degree-of-freedom arm, the
training regime reversed a restriction's verdict, the correction metric
changed frozen-policy outcomes without forwarding a single inadmissible
command, the implemented static systems lost authority that training did not recover,
and admissible commands still produced damage under shifted conditions.
Governors should therefore be declared and tested together with the proposer,
the task information, the correction rule and the specification they enforce.
TRiX makes each of these explicit, which turns execution governance from a
filter certified on its own into a contract whose effect on a robot can be
measured.
