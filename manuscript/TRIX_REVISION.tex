\begin{abstract}
Robotic disassembly needs commands that respect task-specific physical
constraints, yet these increasingly come from learned policies and
vision-language models without such guarantees. Runtime governors that correct
unsafe commands are a natural safeguard, usually assessed in isolation. Here
we present TRiX, an execution governor that allows, projects or rejects each
proposed command against a task-conditioned admissible set, and show that its
value depends on the policy it governs. Across six simulated disassembly
tasks, each with a distinct constraint, the training regime decides whether a
restriction suffices: the same componentwise restriction gives 0\% safe
completion when added to a trained policy and 100\% when present during
training. Yet training cannot supply task context a restriction lacks: where
admissible forces rotate with the part, a static restriction stays at 0\%
while TRiX reaches 49.4\%. TRiX prevents all 31 constraint violations in 273
vision-language-model decisions and corrects commands on robot hardware.
Beyond training conditions, admissible commands still permit damage, marking
the limit of per-step governance.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} robotic disassembly; execution governance; action
projection; robot learning; vision-language models

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Electronic waste is among the fastest-growing waste streams, and recovering
its materials depends on taking products apart without destroying what is
inside \cite{ref1,ref2,ref3,ref4,ref5,ref6}. Automating that work is difficult
because each operation is governed by physical relationships the robot cannot
simply see. A screw must turn as it withdraws, or its thread strips. A latch
must be pressed aside before a cover will lift. A battery pressed too hard can
short internally and keep heating after the robot lets go. Whether a command
is acceptable depends on these relationships and on where the task currently
stands.

These failures differ in kind. Battery deformation that progresses to an
internal short and heating is a hazard; a fractured circuit board, a stripped
thread or a broken latch is a loss of asset integrity. Both can be described
by physical models declared in advance, and this paper measures command
admissibility and task outcomes under such models. Physical safety for a
specific product additionally requires that its models be calibrated against
measured forces, a step the framework introduced here makes explicit.

Robots now have richer sources of candidate actions than ever. Learned
policies and vision-language models (VLMs) can propose manipulation commands
from images, instructions and experience
\cite{ref8,ref9,ref10,ref11,ref12,ref13}, and their roles in reasoning,
planning and interaction for collaborative robotics are developing quickly
\cite{ref39}. These proposers widen what a robot can attempt, but nothing in
them guarantees that a proposed command respects the physics of the part in
front of it. A model can describe the right step and still command a pull
where a turn was needed. Something must decide which proposals reach the
motors.

Existing methods place that decision in different parts of the system.
Constrained reinforcement learning shapes the policy itself under cost limits
\cite{ref16,ref17}, and Lagrangian methods adjust penalties as learning
proceeds \cite{ref18,ref19,ref20}. Safety layers correct actions through a
learned constraint model \cite{ref21}, recovery policies take over when
estimated risk is high \cite{ref22}, and control barrier functions enforce
state-dependent safe sets under explicit dynamical and feasibility assumptions
\cite{ref23,ref24}, with extensions to higher relative degree \cite{ref40} and
time-varying constraints \cite{ref41}. Shields synthesized from temporal-logic
specifications monitor a learner's actions and correct them when needed
\cite{ref42}, optimization layers project network outputs onto constraint
sets during training \cite{ref43}, and predictive safety filters certify a
proposed input over a horizon \cite{ref44}. Table~\ref{tab:submitted-1}
compares representative formulations by where they intervene and the
assumptions behind their guarantees. Whichever mechanism serves as a runtime filter, two factors
outside the filter determine what it achieves. The first is what it is told:
which constraints it represents, and which task information, such as the
current phase or the orientation of the part, it can use. The second is when
the policy meets it. A filter added to a trained policy must correct
proposals that were never shaped by it, whereas a policy trained with the
filter in place, as shields and optimization layers permit
\cite{ref42,ref43}, can learn to work within it. A filter that fails in one
setting can therefore suffice in the other.

We study both factors through TRiX, an execution governor that sits between
the proposer and the robot. Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-1} shows its place in
the command path: every proposal passes through TRiX, and only an approved
command continues to the executor. For each proposed command, TRiX uses the
current task state and a declared task context to select the commands the
task model admits, and it returns one of four decisions. \texttt{ALLOW}
passes a command already in that set; \texttt{PROJECT} replaces a command
outside it with the nearest admissible one; \texttt{REJECT} refuses a request
that no correction can repair; and \texttt{INFEASIBLE} reports that the
specification leaves no usable command. Because the admissible set follows the
task's state and phase, we call TRiX a task-conditioned governor, in contrast
to static restrictions that apply the same limits throughout a task, and we
call the range of commands a restriction leaves available its command
authority. With the contract explicit, the same governor can be evaluated in
both settings that the second factor distinguishes: applied to a policy after
training, and present while the policy learns.

We evaluate TRiX on three foundations, each closer to deployment than the
last. In simulation, DISASM-Bench provides six analytical disassembly tasks
(screw removal, circuit-board extraction, snap-fit release, crank removal,
battery peeling and prying) on which reinforcement-learning policies are
trained across ten seeds with and without the governor. The tasks were chosen
so that each stresses a different kind of constraint: coupled motion in screw
removal, a coupled torque limit in circuit-board extraction, a phase-dependent
latch in snap-fit release, limits that rotate with the part in crank removal,
a delayed internal hazard in battery peeling and a depth-dependent torque
limit in prying. With foundation models, commands proposed by three VLMs from
shared images of the circuit-board task are replayed with and without
governance from identical states. On hardware, a physical robot arm runs the
governor in its command path, so its rejections and corrections change what
the motors execute. The Results follow this order, and the Methods specify
each layer in full.

The results show that a governor cannot be judged apart from the policy it
serves. On circuit-board extraction, the same componentwise restriction yields
0.0\% safe completion when added to a trained policy and 100.0\% when present
during training. Training does not close every gap: on crank removal, where
the admissible forces turn with the part, a static restriction stays at 0.0\%
while TRiX reaches 49.4\%. Given the same constraint on screw removal,
analytical projection and a quadratic program produce identical outcomes,
which separates how a correction is computed from what the governor is told.
Outside the conditions seen in training, snap-fit safe completion under TRiX
falls from 96.5\% to 59.9\%, and latches fracture even though every executed
command stays within its bounds, which shows where per-step governance must be
extended. For commands proposed by VLMs, governance prevents all 31 observed
constraint-violation cases without introducing any, and on the robot arm it
rejects and corrects commands before execution. Execution governance is
therefore best treated as an explicit contract whose value is measured jointly
with the proposer, the specification and the task context.

\section{Results}

The Results follow the three layers. We first summarize the governor and the
evaluation design, then report the simulation benchmark, where controlled
comparisons isolate the training regime, task context, specification,
representation and distribution shift. We then apply the same contract to
commands from foundation models and to a physical robot, and close with its
computational cost.

\subsection{Governor and evaluation design}
\label{sec:design}
Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-1} summarizes the contract introduced above, and the
Methods define it formally. Screw removal shows why the form of the admissible
set matters. Because a screw advances one pitch per turn, admissible commands
couple axial and angular velocity, and the implemented task admits only a
narrow strip of the normalized command space. Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-2}
makes this concrete: a componentwise box cannot represent that strip
(Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-2}a), and clipping can leave a proposal inadmissible
where projection lands on it (Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-2}b). The
same strip can be written as a quadratic program (QP), so solution mechanisms
can be compared with the specification held fixed.

The simulation layer, DISASM-Bench, models six disassembly tasks, abbreviated
SCREW (screw removal), PCB (circuit-board extraction), SNAP (snap-fit
release), CRANK (crank removal), BATTERY (battery peeling) and PRY (prying a
bonded part open), each with its own command interface, constraints and
damage mechanism. Table~\ref{tab:submitted-2} lists these for every task, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:disasm-workspaces} renders each workspace from a recorded
state.

On each task, Proximal Policy
Optimization (PPO) \cite{ref31} and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) \cite{ref32}
policies are trained over ten seeds in two regimes. In Stage~1, a policy is
trained without any restriction and each runtime arm is then applied to the
same frozen policy; we call this post-hoc governance. In Stage~2, a separate policy
is trained with its restriction active; we call this filter-aware training.
Each runtime arm is a restriction placed between policy and environment,
and Table~\ref{tab:submitted-3} gives the role each plays in the
comparisons: the unfiltered reference \texttt{none};
componentwise bounds that ignore coupling (\texttt{box\_clip}) or task phase
(\texttt{static\_clip}); an analytical local-gradient reference
(\texttt{oracle\_tangent}); a quadratic program matched to the screw
constraint (\texttt{qp\_matched}), the form a control-barrier-function filter
takes when its constraint is affine in the command \cite{ref23}; and TRiX. When comparing them we refer to
every arm, TRiX included, as a governor. Any arm may tighten its limits by
a margin that absorbs command noise, and each comparison states which factors
it varies. The primary outcome, safe completion, requires finishing the task
without its declared outcome-affecting violation.

\subsubsection{The training regime decides whether a restriction suffices}
\label{sec:policy-results}
Circuit-board extraction shows most clearly that a governor and its policy act
as one system. Applied after SAC training, the componentwise
\texttt{box\_clip} restriction yields 0.0\% safe completion; with the same
restriction active during training, safe completion is 100.0\%, and all ten
training seeds show the reversal
(Fig.~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}a). The frozen policy is not at fault:
under TRiX it reaches 100.0\%, so the post-hoc failure lies in the
restriction. A policy trained under the box instead learns to work within the
authority the box retains. Because only the regime changes, the regime is
itself a variable that any evaluation of a governor must report.

\subsubsection{Task context, not the solver, separates governors}
\label{sec:context-results}
If the regime can reverse the verdict on a restriction, what else can? Screw
removal rules out the solver. With the constraint held fixed, the analytical
reference (\texttt{oracle\_tangent}), the matched quadratic program
(\texttt{qp\_matched}) and TRiX each reach 88.7\% safe completion for SAC and
60.0\% for PPO under post-hoc governance, against 0.0\% without a governor.
With filter-aware training, the matched constrained controls reach 100\% for
both algorithms. Given the same admissible set, the methods agree exactly.

Task context, by contrast, separates governors even after training. On crank
removal, where the admissible forces turn with the part, the static
restriction stays at 0.0\% after filter-aware SAC training, while the
task-conditioned governor reaches 49.4\%, with seeds clustered near 0\% or
100\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}c). The two governors differ in both
angle-conditioned axial bounds and task-frame transverse representation, so
this pair measures their combined effect. Snap-fit release separates even more
sharply, because a restriction blind to the latch phase cannot distinguish a
premature pull from a permitted one: within the training reset ranges, safe
completion is 5.2\% under the static restriction and 96.5\% under TRiX
(Fig.~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}b). These separately trained
policy-and-governor systems also differ in latch-phase conditioning and
margin, so the gap measures the systems rather than the governor's causal
contribution alone. Under the shifted resets reported below, these rates fall
to 3.5\% and 59.9\%, respectively.

\subsubsection{Simple restrictions suffice when correctly specified}
\label{sec:sufficient-results}
The converse also holds: where a simple restriction already carries the
information a task needs, training makes it sufficient. On prying,
filter-aware SAC reaches 88.5\% under the static restriction and 89.1\% under
the task-conditioned one, with closely aligned seed distributions and the same
zero-performing seed (Fig.~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}d). On circuit-board
extraction in this regime, the box reaches 100.0\% and TRiX 99.8\%. These two
arms also differ in torque margin, a nominal 0.100~N\,m per-axis bound against
a coupled limit tightened for command noise to approximately 0.087087~N\,m,
so the pair measures geometry and margin together rather than box-versus-disk
geometry alone.

Battery peeling shows that the specification can matter more than the form of
the restriction. The original arms compared the 20-N flagged peel boundary
with a 15-N preventive deformation threshold; once componentwise and TRiX arms
share the same preventive target and margin construction, both reach 100\%
safe completion. An exact-arm audit corrected a historical aggregation that
had pooled similarly named BATTERY arms, which sets Stage~1 SAC/TRiX safe
completion to 20.0\%; the original records are retained
(\suppsection{S9.4}). \suppsection{S9} reports the full policy tables and
\suppref{tab:submitted-11} the training-seed intervals and pairing exceptions.
On some tasks the nominal policy itself fails, leaving every frozen-policy arm
at zero, which limits the ranking those cells can support.

\subsubsection{Representation determines retained authority}
\label{sec:representation-results}
All of these differences enter a governor through how its constraints are
represented, which two studies examine directly. On 2,000 held-out
circuit-board proposals, componentwise clipping produces 2.9\% feasible
commands, a learned model linear in the action (action-affine), as in safety layers
\cite{ref21}, 0.0\%, a
learned nonlinear model 78.0\% after one correction and 92.3\% after
iteration, and the analytical oracle and exact projector 100\%. The affine
result stays at 0.0\% as fitting samples grow from 500 to 20,000, which places
its failure in the representation rather than the data. This single-seed study
compares the implemented representations, not learning in general or complete
safety algorithms.

The procedural study widens the comparison to 200 admissible sets drawn from
box, ellipsoid, polytope, state-scaled, rotating and phase-gated families,
each with a required action inside its exact set. A single box that ignores
task context, the context-free box, excludes that action in 124 instances and
retains it in 76. Because the box's own exclusion defines this partition, it
is a constructed calibration rather than an independent test of the box; the
informative results are the other arms (\suppref{tab:procedural-geometry}).
Exact projection retains the required action in 100\% of both groups. A
context-conditioned oracle, which knows the context but is limited to the
largest box inside each set, retains it in 36.3\% and 100\%, respectively, and
the learned affine model in 0\% of both groups, including the group where a
box succeeds. Every arm returns feasible commands on 400 sampled proposals per
instance, partly because the approximate arms share that inscribed box, so
feasibility and retained authority are distinct properties. Models are fitted
separately on each evaluated geometry, so the study measures representation
capacity rather than transfer of one learned model (\suppsection{S10}).

\subsubsection{Per-step admissibility ends at the training boundary}
\label{sec:ood-results}
Every result so far lies within the conditions the policies were trained on.
The prospectively declared shifted-reset evaluation asks what happens beyond
them and shows where per-step governance ends. In the primary outside-support
shell, whose resets are sampled from beyond the training ranges, snap-fit safe
completion is 59.9\% under TRiX and 3.5\% under the static restriction, down
from 96.5\% and 5.2\% in range. As in range, this compares complete
policy-and-governor systems and does not isolate the governor's causal
contribution. Latch fractures, including timeouts, occur in 401 and 468 of
1,000 episodes per arm, and destructive completions, which combine completed
episodes with fracture or accumulated damage and exclude timeouts, number 369
and 886 (\suppref{tab:snap-ood-attribution}). The advantage of TRiX in safe
completion is therefore far larger than its reduction in fractures.

A post-hoc replay audit locates the mechanism. Every TRiX command lies inside
the bounds the governor actually enforced, yet all 401 TRiX fractures begin
through overdeflection among episodes with softer latches, before any pull
damage, while all 480 TRiX episodes with harder latches complete safely.
Admissible commands, applied one step at a time, drove a softer latch into a
damaging state. These counts are observations within the sampled stiffness
intervals, not a safety bound over them; \suppsection{S14.4} reports the full
partition, the traces and how the margins enforced in these runs compare with
a later, tighter margin specification.

The other tasks shift less. Crank removal reaches 47.6\% under TRiX and 0.0\%
under the static restriction, with 22 destructive completions per 1,000
episodes under TRiX, and matched screw arms remain equal at 87.9\% for SAC and
59.9\% for PPO. Circuit-board extraction gives 0.0\%/99.6\% in Stage~1 and
100.0\%/99.8\% in Stage~2 for box/TRiX, and the preventive battery arms give
100.0\%/100.0\%. Prying has a deterministic physical reset and is outside this
evaluation. \suppsection{S14}, \suppref{tab:ood-safe} and
\suppref{tab:ood-outcomes} report all three declared conditions, changes,
intervals and outcome categories; the original in-range results are
unchanged.

\subsection{Governance of foundation-model commands}
\label{sec:vlm-results}
The simulation results concern policies trained on the benchmark. Foundation
models already propose robot actions and programs directly
\cite{ref9,ref10,ref11}, and their outputs reach hardware only through some
execution layer. The second foundation asks whether the same contract governs
commands that no trained policy produced. Three recorded VLM runs received the same 91 circuit-board images
and prompt, giving 273 decisions. A fixed parser maps each response to one of
six motion primitives, such as \texttt{LIFT} or \texttt{TILT\_X}, scaled to a
normalized command; a response it can map is grounded, and one it cannot is
ungroundable. The labels Claude Sonnet, GPT-4o and Gemini identify the
configured aliases and recorded runs; resolved backend versions are not
recorded. The decisions partition into 16 refusals, 219 admissible commands
and 38 inadmissible commands, with no ungroundable output, and each of the 257
grounded commands was replayed with and without governance.
Table~\ref{tab:vlm-results} breaks the decisions and replay outcomes down by
model.

\begin{table}[!htb]

\subsection{Governance on a physical robot}
\label{sec:hardware-results}
The third foundation asks whether the contract changes what physical motors
do. On a Seeed reBot B601-RS arm with a parallel gripper, TRiX sits in the
command path. It rejects a request to move between task phases in an order the
task does not allow, and a proposed $+50^\circ$ motion of Joint~1, which the
contract protects; neither produces a physical target. It projects an
excessive $+50^\circ$ Joint~2 proposal to $+18^\circ$, and only the corrected
target is transmitted and executed. The return that follows, allowed by TRiX,
ends with Joint~1 $+0.007^\circ$ from its starting angle.

The frozen final implementation then completes three video-backed
grasp-and-return trials with the object retained, each with ten
\texttt{ALLOW} decisions, for 30 governed decisions in total;
Fig.~\ref{fig:b601-hardware} follows one trial from approach to return. Across these trials, Joint~1 returns to within
$0.027^\circ$ of its starting angle, and no joint among Joints~1--6 ends more
than $0.122^\circ$ from where it began. Three further logged repetitions
complete normally and are counted separately from the video-backed trials. In
all six, the operator places the object between the open jaws at a calibrated
grasp pose, and the governed sequence closes the gripper, lifts and returns.
The arm carries no independent wrench sensor, so these trials establish the
command boundary on physical hardware; force-level calibration of the six task
models is the next step (\suppsection{S12}).

\subsection{The cost of governance}
\label{sec:runtime-benchmark}
A governor that sits in every command path must also be cheap, and TRiX costs
microseconds per command. We timed 225,280 calls to complete governor
implementations, 22 in all, across six tasks and five fresh processes on an
Intel Core Ultra 9 275HX host. On the shared screw constraint, median and
95th-percentile latencies are 1.255 and 1.556~$\mu$s for the production TRiX
implementation, 12.578 and 15.534~$\mu$s for an active-set QP solver, and
14.924 and 19.895~$\mu$s for the OSQP solver \cite{ref46} reusing its setup
between calls.
The three return the same commands to within $1.013\times10^{-6}$ in any
normalized coordinate and pass the declared agreement checks. Across the TRiX
implementations for the six tasks, median latency ranges from 1.123 to
11.770~$\mu$s. Implementation matters as much as formulation: a NumPy
analytical implementation of the same geometry takes 18.163~$\mu$s by median,
slower than both QP solvers. \suppref{tab:runtime-latency} reports every
implementation and its process variation. All outliers are retained, and these
are measured costs on the recorded host, not worst-case or end-to-end robot
latency.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The Introduction asked how two factors outside a governor, when the policy
meets it and what it is told, shape what it achieves. Both matter. A runtime governor is usually presented as
a component that can be judged on its own; our central result is that it
cannot. The same restriction on circuit-board extraction fails as a post-hoc
filter and succeeds as a training-time constraint, so a governor's value is a
property of the system it joins. Shields and optimization layers can already act while a policy learns
\cite{ref42,ref43}; our results show that this choice can reverse a
restriction's verdict. Evaluations should therefore report post-hoc
governance and filter-aware learning separately, because each supports a
different claim.

Within that system, what the governor is told decides the outcome. On crank
removal, no amount of training supplies the context a static restriction
omits. Given the same command set, analytical projection and a QP coincide on
screw removal; given the same preventive specification, componentwise and TRiX
arms coincide on battery peeling. The procedural study separates two properties
that are easily conflated: a correction can be feasible and still discard the
action the task requires. Learned representations of the kind used in safety layers \cite{ref21} are
not ruled out, since the iterated nonlinear model recovers most of the
circuit-board constraint. The
contribution is therefore an auditable execution contract and an experimental
map of when each kind of restriction suffices, rather than a universal ranking
of analytical projection over learned or classical methods. The timing results
point the same way: the speed advantage belongs to the scalar implementation,
since the NumPy analytical version is slower than both matched QP controls.

The experiments also show where per-step governance ends, and that boundary
defines what must be built next. Outside training support, every TRiX command
on snap-fit release stayed within bounds, yet fractures fell only from 468 to
401 per 1,000 episodes: admissible commands do not by themselves secure the
state they produce. In battery peeling, an internal short can keep heating
after force is removed, beyond the reach of any filter on current commands. In
snap-fit release, suppressing a premature pull does not supply the latch
deflection the task needs. A correct projector also enforces whatever boundary
it is given, so the 20-N flagged battery limit allows deformation that begins
at 15~N. Finally, probabilistic tightening can remove authority altogether. To
absorb command noise, each limit $L$ is tightened by $z$ noise standard
deviations $\sigma$, which leaves usable commands only while $z\sigma<L$. For
the crank's axial limit, $L/\sigma=5/1.235\simeq4.05$, below the declared
multiplier of approximately 4.488, so the tightened limit admits no command, a
specification-feasibility failure detailed in \suppsection{S13}. Each of these
limits points to a specific extension: state-aware constraints of the kind
control barrier functions provide \cite{ref23,ref40}, sequence-level
certification as in predictive safety filters \cite{ref44}, and calibrated
specifications.

These boundaries are empirical, and the study reports them in full. When 21
model constants are perturbed, the ordering of governors predicted for each
task holds in 96 of 105 cases; the nine exceptions comprise six PCB boundary
or model cases, one CRANK feasibility failure and two BATTERY completion
trade-offs with zero destructive completions in both arms
(\suppsection{S7}). A separately preregistered bayonet-mount task, intended as
a prospective test, is inconclusive because neither policy algorithm reached
its predeclared competence gate, the minimum safe completion required before
its governors are compared, and it is reported as such.

Across its three foundations, the study carries one execution contract from
learned policies to foundation-model proposals to physical motors. The model
study shows governance intercepting real generative commands, on
visibility-scaled images of one task, and shows that different models fail on
different inputs. The hardware study shows rejection and projection changing
what physical actuators execute and supporting repeated manipulation, with a
calibrated grasp, manual object placement and a separate joint-space contract.
Neither calibrates the benchmark's force, torque or damage thresholds; that
requires independent physical measurements across contact uncertainty, object
variation and model mismatch. The natural next step is to join the three
layers: calibrated force and damage models, state estimation and sequence
planning, measured on hardware against the same contract. Execution governance
is most informative when its proposal distribution, task context and physical
specification are evaluated together, and TRiX makes each of them explicit.

\section{Methods}

The Methods specify the execution contract and each evaluation layer in the
order the Results use them, followed by measurement, statistics and
provenance.

\subsection{The execution contract}
\label{sec:authority}
Throughout, $x$ denotes the observed task state, $c$ the declared task
context, such as the current phase of a snap-fit release, $\tilde u$ a
proposed command and $u$ the command TRiX forwards, both in normalized
coordinates with each component in $[-1,1]$, and $\mathcal A(x,c)$ the
admissible set; Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-1} uses the same symbols. TRiX
receives $\tilde u$ together with $x$ and $c$. From these it selects the admissible command set $\mathcal A(x,c)$
and returns one of four decisions. \texttt{ALLOW} forwards a proposal already
in the set. \texttt{PROJECT} replaces an inadmissible proposal with a feasible
correction. \texttt{REJECT} refuses a request that breaks the task's sequence or state
rules, such as a move between task phases the task does not allow, which no
numerical correction can repair. \texttt{INFEASIBLE} reports that the selected
specification leaves no usable command. Only the first two forward
a command for execution. When $\mathcal A(x,c)$ is a nonempty closed convex
set in normalized command coordinates, the correction is the unique Euclidean
projection
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathcal A}(\tilde u)=\arg\min_{u\in\mathcal A(x,c)}
\tfrac12\|u-\tilde u\|_2^2.
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
The normalization is itself part of the model: velocity, angular-velocity and
force errors share this metric only after each has a declared scale.

Screw removal shows why the representation of $\mathcal A$ matters. A screw
of pitch $p$ advances one pitch per turn, so ideal motion couples axial
velocity $v_z$ to angular velocity $\omega_z$ through $v_z=k\omega_z$, where
$k=p/(2\pi)$. Writing a command's normalized axial and angular components as
$a_v=v_z/S_v$ and $a_\omega=\omega_z/S_\omega$, as in
Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-2}, a proposal is $\tilde u=(\tilde a_v,\tilde
a_\omega)$, and with coupling coefficient $\alpha=kS_\omega/S_v$ its
projection onto the ideal line is
\begin{equation}
\Pi_0(\tilde u)=
\frac{\alpha\tilde a_v+\tilde a_\omega}{1+\alpha^2}
\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:helix}
\end{equation}
No pair of independent bounds on $a_v$ and $a_\omega$ can express this
coupling. The implemented SCREW task widens the line into a finite-tolerance
strip that occupies 2.0\% of the normalized command slice in
Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-2}a, bounded by command and radial-force limits.
Componentwise clipping can leave a proposal outside that strip, whereas
projection lands on it (Fig.~\ref{fig:submitted-2}b). A suitably specified
linear-constraint quadratic program (QP) represents the same set, which lets
the experiments separate the solution mechanism from the constraint
representation.

Four properties of command sets frame the experiments. Projection onto an
axis-aligned product of intervals reduces to componentwise clipping. A
phase-independent set valid in two phases must lie within their intersection
and may therefore discard authority that one phase allows. The intersection
of a rectangle with half-widths $a\geq b>0$ over all rotations is the disk of
radius $b$, so a fixed square $[-h,h]^2$ valid at every orientation requires
$h\leq b/\sqrt2$. A Gaussian-tightened interval $|y|\leq L-z\sigma$ keeps
nonzero authority exactly when $z\sigma<L$; the Supplementary Information
gives the assumptions and proofs (\suppsection{S3}). These
properties describe what a command set can represent and when it retains
authority; closed-loop stability and forward invariance for an unmodeled plant
require dynamical analysis beyond this contract. How a trained policy adapts
to a restriction is measured empirically rather than stated as a proposition.

\subsection{DISASM-Bench tasks and outcomes}
\label{sec:tasks}
DISASM-Bench turns six common disassembly operations into analytical models
with declared constraints and damage mechanisms: SCREW (screw removal), PCB
(circuit-board extraction), SNAP (snap-fit release), CRANK (crank removal),
BATTERY (battery peeling) and PRY (prying a bonded part open with a lever).
Each task maps three normalized action coordinates to its own physical
interface (Table~\ref{tab:submitted-2}). SCREW commands axial and angular
velocity $v_z^c,\omega_z^c$ and radial force $F_r$; PCB commands tilt torques
$\tau_x,\tau_y$ and lift force $F_z$; SNAP commands latch-deflection, pull and
lateral forces $F_{\rm def},F_{\rm pull},F_{\rm lat}$; CRANK commands forces
$F_x,F_y,F_z$; BATTERY commands peel force $F_{\rm peel}$ and lateral forces
$F_{\ell1},F_{\ell2}$; and PRY commands tool torque $\tau$, insertion force
$F_{\rm ins}$ and lateral force $F_{\rm lat}$, with a torque limit that
depends on insertion depth $d$.

A versioned registry binds each task model, SCREW v3 and the five v2 tasks
of Table~\ref{tab:submitted-2}, to its environment and named filters. Every observation has ten float32 entries
whose meanings are task-specific, with unused entries padded with zeros; PCB,
for example, observes lift and lift velocity, two tilt angles and their rates,
accumulated damage and a fracture flag. Actions are normalized to $[-1,1]^3$
and mapped through the physical scales in \suppsection{S4}. Each outer action
advances $1/240$~s of simulated time, integrated with 40 internal substeps for
SCREW, ten for PCB, SNAP, CRANK and BATTERY, and directly for PRY. Progress
and termination are task-specific.

Every episode ends in one outcome defined by a single contract. Safe
completion requires successful task termination without the task's declared
outcome-affecting violation. The contract separates it from completion with
such a violation, from destructive completion and from the remaining
unsuccessful outcomes, and it records diagnostic events separately. Reset
parameters, their distributions and ranges, and whether each is used for
training or evaluation are tabulated in \suppref{tab:submitted-9} and
detailed in \suppsection{S4.6}; risk allocations, noise and reset variation
all come from the frozen task code.

The analytical environments generate every reported dynamic and outcome.
Fig.~\ref{fig:disasm-workspaces} renders their recorded states in PyBullet
\cite{ref33} from a per-panel state manifest, for visualization only: the
illustrated gripper shows the command interface, while arm and contact
dynamics lie outside the task models. An earlier PyBullet prototype imposed
the screw pitch through a gear constraint and therefore could not validate
that pitch independently; we withdrew the associated validation argument.

\subsection{Policies, regimes and comparators}
\label{sec:training-methods}
We train two standard reinforcement-learning algorithms, Proximal Policy
Optimization (PPO) \cite{ref31} and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) \cite{ref32},
with Stable-Baselines3 2.9.0 \cite{ref45}, Gymnasium 1.3.0 and two-layer $[256,256]$
networks on CPU, using ten independent training seeds, 0--9, for every
comparison. The two evaluation regimes mirror the two ways a governor can meet
a policy. In Stage~1, a policy is trained without the evaluated governor, a
checkpoint is selected, and each runtime arm in Table~\ref{tab:submitted-3} is
applied to that same frozen policy. In Stage~2, a separate policy is trained
with its restriction inside the environment transition, while replay and
rollout buffers retain the nominal policy action. The training adapter
separates termination from time-limit truncation and restores NumPy random
state after interleaved evaluation.

A predeclared rule selects checkpoints so that no arm benefits from tuning.
At each task budget, checkpoints saved every 1,000 steps are screened on 30
fixed validation episodes. Three-checkpoint windows are ranked by mean safe
completion, and the centers of the three best windows receive 70 additional
validation episodes. Selection is lexicographic: highest safe completion, then
lowest destructive completion, highest normalized progress and lowest
episode-level violation incidence, with the earliest checkpoint breaking any
remaining tie. Each selected policy is tested on 100 held-out episodes
disjoint from validation, and pilot seeds are excluded. Checkpoint lists,
software fingerprints, task budgets and episode-seed exceptions are retained
in \suppsection{S5} and \suppsection{S6}.

The runtime arms are chosen so that each comparison changes one factor where
possible and states what it changes where not (Table~\ref{tab:submitted-3}).
Componentwise restrictions, analytical local-gradient references, a matched
QP and the task-specific TRiX operators form the registry. The quantitative
policy matrix contains only PPO and SAC task policies. It includes neither a
PPO-Lagrangian policy, which adds a learned cost penalty to PPO, nor a policy
trained with the learned safety layer of ref.~\cite{ref21} (SafeLayer);
learned constraint representations are studied separately below. The literature methods in
Table~\ref{tab:submitted-1} keep their original assumptions and are
positioned conceptually rather than run as experimental baselines.

Two controls hold the specification fixed. For SCREW, the matched QP and the
analytical solver minimize the same normalized Euclidean error over the same
finite helical slab, action bounds and radial limit, so the comparison
isolates solution mechanism; state-space barrier synthesis is a separate
construction. For BATTERY, the preventive arms share the 15-N threshold and
margin construction. Other pairs vary more than one factor, and we report them
that way: the PCB and SNAP arms retain their implemented margin differences,
and CRANK combines frame and angle conditioning. Historical BATTERY prefix
pooling and the PCB oracle episode-seed correction are documented alongside
preserved originals. The full policy tables use exact-arm counts, and no
checkpoint was reselected during that audit.

\subsection{Representation, shift and sensitivity studies}
\label{sec:model-validation}
Four studies stress the benchmark beyond the main policy comparisons. Two
examine constraint representation directly, apart from trained policies. The
PCB decomposition uses the archived seed-0 run, fitting learned models on
20,000 samples and testing them on 2,000 held-out proposals, together with an
affine-model sweep over 500--20,000 fitting samples. The procedural benchmark
generates six families of three-dimensional admissible sets, with 120
training, 40 validation and 200 held-out geometries from disjoint seed
streams. Learned constraint models are fitted on each evaluated instance from
3,000 labeled state-action samples, with 250 gradient steps for the nonlinear
model, and every arm receives 400 sampled proposals per instance. An arm
retains the required action when its displacement stays below
$0.02(\|u_{\rm req}\|+10^{-9})$. The context-free box's own exclusion of the
required action defines the 124/76 partition, and the approximate arms share
its inscribed-box bounds, which can contribute both feasibility and
conservatism.

The third study tests behavior beyond the training conditions. The original
held-out tests use the training reset ranges, and a prospectively declared
evaluation adds 42,000 episodes across the five tasks with stochastic physical
resets. It widens
each randomized reset range about its centre to $1.5\times$ and $2\times$ its
half-width and adds a $2\times$ outside-support shell that samples resets only
from outside the training support, keeping selected policies and governors
fixed. PRY is not applicable because its physical reset is
deterministic. \suppsection{S14} and \suppref{tab:ood-ranges} give the
committed protocol, explicit reset interface and complete ranges. The SNAP
command and damage attribution reported in Results was conducted after this
evaluation and is labelled post hoc.

The fourth study perturbs the task models themselves. A sensitivity sweep
varies 21 constants across five multipliers from 0.5 to 2.0, with eight
scripted episodes per arm and case. It orders arms first by destructive
completions and, when both are zero, by safe completion, and its nine failed
cases are diagnosed after the sweep. Separately, a bayonet-mount release
task (BAYONET), preregistered as a prospective test, fixes a 150,000-step
budget, SAC as the primary and PPO as a replication algorithm, and a 50\%
competence gate, the minimum safe completion required before its governors
are compared. Neither algorithm exceeds
10\% mean safe completion, and the gate and task were left unchanged after
this inconclusive result. \suppsection{S7} and \suppsection{S10} give
implementation checks, designs and full outcomes.

\subsection{Foundation-model commands}
\label{sec:vlm-method}
The foundation-model layer asks whether TRiX governs the commands that real
models produce. A frozen bank of 91 images covers 28 base configurations of
the PCB task: 21 appear in normal, rotated, occluded and misleading views, and
seven in one partial view each. Each provider received the same image and
prompt pairs with a 2,000-token output limit. The recorded aliases are
\texttt{claude-sonnet-4-5}, \texttt{gpt-4o} and \texttt{gemini-flash-latest};
they are not pinned backend snapshots \cite{ref36,ref37,ref38}. All raw
responses and parsed commands are retained, while backend IDs, per-call dates
and retry attribution are unavailable. Historical notes mention four retried
Gemini timeouts whose image IDs and successful variants cannot be recovered,
and the client also permits fallback outside those events.

The images make tilt visible by scaling it. In the 84 main-stratum images,
the 21 configurations shown in four views, display tilt is approximately
$20.669$ times the replayed mechanical curvature, and partial
views use $3.6$ times. Mechanical tilts saved for states near the
fracture boundary, 0.0358--0.042525~rad, therefore appear as roughly $42.4^\circ$--$50.4^\circ$,
beyond the model's $0.075$-rad ($4.30^\circ$) fracture threshold if read
physically, so a displayed pose need not be a realizable intact-board state.
Positive scaling preserves ordering within the 84 main-stratum images but not
physical distance to a boundary, and all margin comparisons exclude the seven
partial views. Rendering reproducibility does not establish fidelity to
mechanical tilt. The stratum counts describe responses to this encoding, and
without an unscaled image control, effects of visual scaling cannot be
separated from differences between model runs.

A fixed parser maps each output to \texttt{LIFT}, \texttt{TILT\_X},
\texttt{TILT\_Y}, \texttt{PRESS}, \texttt{HOLD} or \texttt{ABORT}. Refusals
and ungroundable outputs are counted separately from grounded admissible and
inadmissible commands, and set membership alone defines inadmissibility, since
no independent semantic-correctness annotation is available. Each grounded
command is held for 40 steps in unfiltered and governed replay from the same
restored state and seed. A violation case contains at least one recorded
constraint violation, and prevention requires violations only in the
unfiltered arm. \suppsection{S11} gives the display formulas, trial ledger,
stratum counts and a conditional Gemini sensitivity bound.

\subsection{Physical platform}
\label{sec:hardware-methods}
The hardware layer places TRiX in the command path of a Seeed reBot B601-RS
arm with RobStride actuators, a 48-V supply and 1-Mbit/s CAN. Joints~1--5
receive 0.1-second position/velocity commands in the actuators' MIT
control mode, and Joint~6 runs an
approximately 200-Hz velocity controller. TRiX keeps proposed task-level actions,
logged decisions and approved joint targets separate, and protects Joint~1
with a hard invariant that rejects proposals to move it. The final grasp implementation is identified by commit
\texttt{d296e6e} and tag \path{trix-hardware-soft-grasp-success-20260828}; its
calibrated sequence uses gravity feed-forward on Joint~3 and a disclosed Joint~2
positioning offset around engagement. In all six grasp-and-return trials, the
operator manually positions the object between the open jaws at the
calibrated grasp-ready pose before governed closure, lift and return. The
placement protocol for the three repetitions without video is
author-confirmed, and their logs record robot execution. Console logs,
passive CAN captures, source and configuration snapshots, video mappings and
checksums support the intervention experiment, the three video-backed trials
and the three logged repetitions. The platform has no independent
force/torque measurement and no autonomous perception-to-grasp stage
(\suppsection{S12}).

\subsection{Measurement, statistics and provenance}
\label{sec:statistics}
To measure what governance costs per command, the latency benchmark times the
complete governor call $(x,\tilde u)\mapsto(u,\mathrm{info})$, from observation and
proposal to approved command and diagnostics, including allocations and
per-call constraint construction. It uses 2,048 saved inputs
per task, five fresh processes and 256 warm-up calls per
implementation/process. Every arm sees the same per-task input sequence, with
task/arm order randomized in 64-input blocks. Durations are untrimmed integer
nanoseconds, cyclic garbage collection is disabled, and timer overhead is
recorded without subtraction. Each process uses CPU~0 and one native
numerical thread. OSQP reuses its problem and warm start, and its one-time
construction is reported separately. Policy/VLM inference, input generation,
simulation and robot communication fall outside the interval.
\suppsection{S8} records host and software versions, tolerances, raw arrays,
source hashes and the complete workload design.

The training seed, not the evaluation episode, is the experimental unit for
policy comparisons. We report ten-seed means together with every plotted seed
value. Selected contrasts use paired differences by seed identifier and exact
percentile bootstrap distributions over those ten differences, computed by
convolution; the intervals are descriptive, not multiplicity-adjusted tests.
Same-policy Stage~1 contrasts are kept distinct from separately trained
Stage~2 and cross-regime pairs. The preventive BATTERY and PCB cross-regime
contrasts also have recorded episode-seed mismatches, so they are paired only
at training-seed level (\suppref{tab:submitted-11}). The shifted reset study
uses the same estimator and training-seed pairing; its changes from historical
in-range estimates include episode-sampling variation because the schedules
differ, and within/outside-support strata are descriptive
(\suppsection{S14}). Procedural instances are the units of the geometry
study. VLM counts are descriptive over paired images and commands, views
clustered within 28 configurations are not independent physical replicates,
and no inferential cross-model test is reported.

Every experimental record is linked to the applicable environment, policy,
constraint, margin and outcome specifications, and a curated source map links
all main and supplementary figures and tables to data or executable source.
Correction notes retain affected historical files. Independent physical
calibration and unresolved backend identities remain provenance limits.
Fig.~\ref{fig:seed-distributions} was regenerated from the retained
seed-level data with a supplied replacement plotting script; the original
plotting script remains unavailable.
