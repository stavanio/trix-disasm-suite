\begin{abstract}
Recovering materials from electronic waste requires robots to take products
apart without damaging the parts inside. Robots now receive candidate actions
from learned policies and foundation models, so something must decide which
reach the motors. Here we show that the value of such an execution
governor depends on the policy it governs. We present TRiX, which passes,
corrects or refuses each proposed command using constraints tied to the task's
current state, and evaluate it across simulation, real vision-language-model
outputs and physical hardware. A simple filter that permits no safe extraction
when added to a trained policy permits safe extraction every time when present
during training, while task information missing from a filter cannot be
learned around. Governance intercepts every recorded constraint violation in
model commands and corrects commands on a physical arm before execution.
Outside training conditions, correctly enforced constraints still permit
latch damage, marking the limit of per-step governance. Governors must be
evaluated with the policies they serve.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} robotic disassembly; execution governance; action
projection; robot learning; vision-language models

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Electronic waste is among the fastest-growing waste streams, and recovering
its materials depends on taking products apart without destroying what is
inside \cite{ref1,ref2,ref3,ref4,ref5,ref6}. Automating that work is difficult
because each operation is governed by physical relationships the robot cannot
simply see. A screw must turn as it withdraws, or its thread strips. A latch
must be pressed aside before a cover will lift. A battery pressed too hard can
short internally and keep heating after the robot lets go. Whether a command
is acceptable depends on these relationships and on where the task currently
stands.

Robots now have far richer sources of candidate actions. Learned policies and
vision-language models (VLMs) can propose manipulation commands from images,
instructions and experience \cite{ref8,ref9,ref10,ref11,ref12,ref13}, and
their roles in reasoning, planning and interaction for collaborative robotics
are developing quickly \cite{ref39}. These proposers widen what a robot can
attempt, but nothing in them guarantees that a proposed command respects the
physics of the part in front of it. A model can describe the right step and
still command a pull where a turn was needed. Something must decide which
proposals are allowed to reach the motors.

Established methods place that decision in different locations
(Table~\ref{tab:submitted-1}). Constrained reinforcement learning shapes the
policy itself under cost limits \cite{ref16,ref17}, and Lagrangian methods
adjust penalties as learning proceeds \cite{ref18,ref19,ref20}. Safety layers
correct actions through a learned constraint model \cite{ref21}, recovery
policies take over when estimated risk is high \cite{ref22}, and control
barrier functions enforce state-dependent safe sets under explicit dynamical
and feasibility assumptions \cite{ref23,ref24}, with extensions to higher
relative degree \cite{ref40} and time-varying constraints \cite{ref41}. When
such a mechanism acts as a runtime filter, two factors outside the filter
shape what it achieves. The first is what it is told: which constraints it
represents, and which task information, such as the current phase or the
orientation of the part, it can use. The second is when the policy meets it.
A filter added to an already trained policy must correct proposals that were
never shaped by it, whereas a policy trained with the filter in place can
learn to work within it. A filter that looks inadequate in one setting can be
sufficient in the other.

Two kinds of failure matter in this setting. Battery deformation that
progresses to an internal short and heating is a hazard; a fractured circuit
board, a stripped thread or a broken latch is a loss of asset integrity. TRiX
enforces the constraints of declared physical models of both, and our
measurements report command admissibility and task outcomes under those
models. Physical safety for a specific product additionally requires that its
models be calibrated against measured forces, a step the framework makes
explicit.

This paper studies both factors through TRiX, an execution governor placed
between the proposer and the robot (Figure~\ref{fig:submitted-1}). For each
proposed command, TRiX uses the current task state and declared task context
to select the set of commands the task model admits. It passes a command
already in that set, projects one outside it onto the nearest admissible
command, refuses one that cannot be meaningfully corrected, and reports when
the specification leaves no usable command at all. Only passed or corrected
commands reach the executor. Because the governor is explicit about what it
represents and when it acts, the same governor can be evaluated in both
settings: applied to a policy after training, and present while the policy
learns.

We evaluate TRiX on three foundations, each closer to deployment than the
last. In simulation, DISASM-Bench provides six analytical disassembly tasks,
screw removal, circuit-board extraction, snap-fit release, crank removal,
battery peeling and prying, on which reinforcement-learning policies are
trained across ten seeds with and without the governor. With foundation
models, commands proposed by three VLMs from shared images of the
circuit-board task are replayed with and without governance from identical
states. On hardware, a physical robot arm runs the governor in its command
path, so that rejections and corrections change what the motors actually
execute. Together these layers test one execution contract against learned
proposers, generative proposers and real actuation.

The results show that a governor cannot be judged apart from the policy it
serves. On circuit-board extraction, the same simple restriction yields
0.0\% safe completion when added to a trained policy and 100.0\% when present
during training. Learning does not fill every gap: on crank removal, where the
admissible forces turn with the part, a fixed restriction stays at 0.0\%
while the task-conditioned governor reaches 49.4\%. When analytical projection
and a quadratic program are given the same constraint on screw removal, their
outcomes are identical, which separates how a correction is computed from what
the governor is told. Outside the conditions seen in training, snap-fit safe
completion under TRiX falls from 96.5\% to 59.9\%, and latches fracture even
though every executed command stays within its bounds, which locates precisely
where per-step governance must be extended. For commands proposed by VLMs,
governance prevents all 31 observed constraint-violation cases without
introducing any, and on the robot arm it rejects and corrects commands before
they are executed. Together, these findings establish execution governance as
an explicit contract whose value is measured jointly with the proposer, the
specification and the task context.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Execution contract}
\label{sec:authority}
Let $x$ be the observed state, $c$ the declared task context and
$\mathcal A(x,c)$ the applicable admissible command set. A proposer supplies a
command $\tilde a$. TRiX returns \texttt{ALLOW} for a proposal already in that
set, \texttt{PROJECT} for an inadmissible proposal with a feasible correction,
\texttt{REJECT} for a non-projectable symbolic or task-state violation, and
\texttt{INFEASIBLE} when the selected specification supplies no usable
command authority. Rejection and infeasibility do not forward a command for
execution. For a nonempty closed convex set in normalized command coordinates,
the correction is the unique Euclidean projection
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{\mathcal A}(\tilde a)=\arg\min_{a\in\mathcal A(x,c)}
\tfrac12\|a-\tilde a\|_2^2.
\label{eq:projection}
\end{equation}
Normalization is part of the model: squared velocity, angular-velocity and
force errors require declared scales before they can share this metric.

Screw extraction shows why the representation of $\mathcal A$ matters. With
pitch $p$, axial velocity $v_z$ and angular velocity $\omega_z$, ideal motion
obeys $v_z=k\omega_z$, where $k=p/(2\pi)$. Using command scales $S_v$ and
$S_\omega$ gives normalized commands $a_v=v_z/S_v$ and
$a_\omega=\omega_z/S_\omega$, and a coupling coefficient
$\alpha=kS_\omega/S_v$. The projection onto the ideal line is
\begin{equation}
\Pi_0(\tilde a)=
\frac{\alpha\tilde a_v+\tilde a_\omega}{1+\alpha^2}
\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:helix}
\end{equation}
Independent coordinate bounds do not encode this coupling. The implemented
SCREW task uses a finite-tolerance strip with command and radial-force bounds
(Figure~\ref{fig:submitted-2}); an appropriately specified linear-constraint
quadratic program (QP) represents the same set. This makes solution mechanism
separable from constraint representation.

Four representation properties frame the analysis. First, projection onto an
axis-aligned product of intervals is componentwise clipping. Second, a
phase-independent set valid in two phases must lie in their intersection,
which may discard phase-specific authority. Third, the intersection over all
rotations of a rectangle with half-widths $a\geq b>0$ is the radius-$b$ disk;
a fixed square $[-h,h]^2$ valid for every orientation therefore requires
$h\leq b/\sqrt2$. Fourth, a Gaussian-tightened scalar interval
$|y|\leq L-z\sigma$ retains nonzero authority exactly when $z\sigma<L$.
The assumptions and proofs are in \suppsection{S1}, \suppsection{S2} and
\suppsection{S3}. These properties characterize what a command set can
represent and when it retains authority; closed-loop stability and forward
invariance for an unmodeled plant require dynamical analysis beyond this
contract. How a trained policy adapts to a restriction is measured
empirically rather than stated as a proposition.

\subsection{Disassembly tasks and outcome definitions}
\label{sec:tasks}
DISASM-Bench supplies six analytical environments: SCREW (screw extraction),
PCB (circuit-board extraction), SNAP (snap-fit release), CRANK (crank
extraction), BATTERY (battery peel) and PRY (prying a bonded part open with a lever). Each
maps three normalized action coordinates to a task-specific physical
interface (Table~\ref{tab:submitted-2}). The commanded quantities are axial
and angular velocity $v_z^c,\omega_z^c$ and radial force $F_r$ for SCREW;
tilt torques $\tau_x,\tau_y$ and lift force $F_z$ for PCB; latch-deflection,
pull and lateral forces $F_{\rm def},F_{\rm pull},F_{\rm lat}$ for SNAP;
forces $F_x,F_y,F_z$ for CRANK; peel force $F_{\rm peel}$ and lateral forces
$F_{\ell1},F_{\ell2}$ for BATTERY; and tool torque $\tau$, insertion force
$F_{\rm ins}$ and lateral force $F_{\rm lat}$ for PRY, whose torque limit
depends on insertion depth $d$.

declared outcome-affecting violation. The outcome contract also distinguishes
completion with outcome-affecting violations, destructive completion and the

\subsection{Policies and evaluation regimes}
\label{sec:training-methods}
We train Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \cite{ref31} and Soft
Actor-Critic (SAC) \cite{ref32} policies with Stable-Baselines3 2.9.0,
Gymnasium 1.3.0 and two-layer $[256,256]$ networks on CPU. Ten independent
training seeds, 0--9, define each policy comparison. Stage~1 trains without
the evaluated governor and then applies each runtime arm in
Table~\ref{tab:submitted-3} to the same selected frozen policy. Stage~2 trains
a separate policy with its restriction inside the environment transition;
replay and rollout buffers retain the nominal policy action. The adapter
distinguishes termination from time-limit truncation and restores NumPy
random state after interleaved evaluation.

At each predeclared task budget, checkpoints saved every 1,000 steps are
screened on 30 fixed validation episodes. Three-checkpoint windows are ranked
by mean safe completion, and the centers of the three best windows receive
70 additional validation episodes. Selection is lexicographic: maximize safe
completion, minimize destructive completion, maximize normalized progress,
minimize episode-level violation incidence, then choose the earliest
checkpoint. Each selected policy is tested on 100 held-out episodes, disjoint
from validation; pilot seeds are excluded. Recorded checkpoint lists,
software fingerprints, task budgets and episode-seed exceptions are retained
in \suppsection{S5} and \suppsection{S6}.

\subsection{Representation, sensitivity and prospective tests}
\label{sec:model-validation}
The PCB decomposition uses the archived seed-0 run with 20,000 fitting
samples and 2,000 held-out proposals, plus the 500--20,000-sample
affine-model sweep. The procedural generator defines six three-dimensional
set families, with 120 training, 40 validation and 200 held-out geometries
from disjoint seed streams. Learned constraint models are fitted separately
on each evaluated instance using 3,000 labeled state-action samples;
nonlinear fitting uses 250 gradient steps. Each arm receives 400 sampled
proposals per instance. Required-action retention means displacement below
$0.02(\|u_{\rm req}\|+10^{-9})$. The context-free box's required-action
exclusion defines the 124/76 partition. Approximate arms share implemented
inscribed-box bounds, which can contribute both feasibility and conservatism.

The sensitivity study varies 21 constants across five multipliers from 0.5
to 2.0, with eight scripted episodes per arm and case. Its ordering criterion
first compares destructive completions and, when both are zero, safe
completion. The nine failed cases are diagnosed after the sweep. The separate
BAYONET experiment preregisters a 150,000-step budget, SAC as the primary and
PPO as a replication algorithm, and a 50\% competence gate; neither exceeds
10\% mean safe completion. The gate and task are not changed after this
inconclusive result. \suppsection{S7} and \suppsection{S10} give
implementation checks, designs and full outcomes.

\subsection{Shifted reset evaluation}
\label{sec:ood-methods}
Held-out tests in the original evaluation use the training reset ranges.
A prospectively declared evaluation adds 42,000 episodes across the five
tasks with stochastic physical resets. It widens existing reset half-widths
by $1.5\times$ and $2\times$ and samples a $2\times$ outside-support shell,
with selected policies and governors fixed. PRY is not applicable because its
physical reset is deterministic. \suppsection{S14} and \suppref{tab:ood-ranges}
give the committed protocol, explicit reset interface and complete ranges.
The SNAP command and damage attribution reported in Results was conducted
after this evaluation and is labelled post hoc.

\subsection{Recorded vision-language-model commands}
\label{sec:vlm-method}
The frozen bank has 28 base configurations: 21 have normal, rotated,
occluded and misleading views, and seven have one partial view each,
producing 91 images. Each provider receives the same image/prompt pairs and
a 2,000-token output limit. Recorded aliases are
\texttt{claude-sonnet-4-5}, \texttt{gpt-4o} and
\texttt{gemini-flash-latest}; they are not pinned backend snapshots
\cite{ref36,ref37,ref38}. All raw responses and parsed commands are retained.
Backend IDs, per-call dates and retry attribution are unavailable. Four
retried Gemini timeouts are mentioned in historical notes, but their image
IDs and successful variants cannot be recovered; the client also permits
fallback outside those events.

The images use a visibility-scaled tilt cue. Main-stratum display tilt is
approximately $20.669$ times replayed mechanical curvature; partial views use
$3.6$ times. Saved near-boundary mechanical tilts of 0.0358--0.042525~rad
appear as roughly $42.4^\circ$--$50.4^\circ$, beyond the model's $0.075$-rad
($4.30^\circ$) fracture threshold if interpreted physically. Thus the
displayed pose need not be a realizable intact-board state. Positive scaling
preserves ordering within the 84 main-stratum images, but not physical
distance to a boundary; all margin comparisons exclude the seven partial
views. Rendering reproducibility does not establish fidelity to mechanical
tilt. The stratum counts describe responses to this encoding. Without an
unscaled image control, effects of visual scaling cannot be separated from
differences between model runs.

The fixed parser maps outputs to \texttt{LIFT}, \texttt{TILT\_X},
\texttt{TILT\_Y}, \texttt{PRESS}, \texttt{HOLD} or \texttt{ABORT}.
Refusals and ungroundable outputs are distinct from grounded admissible and
inadmissible commands. Set membership defines inadmissibility; no independent
semantic-correctness annotation is available. Each grounded command is held
for 40 steps in unfiltered and governed replay from the same restored state
and seed. A violation case contains at least one recorded constraint
violation; prevention requires violations only in the unfiltered arm.
\suppsection{S11} provides the display formulas, trial ledger, stratum counts
and conditional Gemini sensitivity bound.

\subsection{Physical platform}
\label{sec:hardware-methods}
The Seeed reBot B601-RS uses RobStride actuators, a 48-V supply and
1-Mbit/s CAN. Joints~1--5 use 0.1-second MIT-style position/velocity
commands; Joint~6 uses an approximately 200-Hz velocity controller. The final
grasp implementation is identified by commit \texttt{d296e6e} and tag
\path{trix-hardware-soft-grasp-success-20260828}. TRiX separates symbolic
proposals, decision receipts and approved executor targets, with Joint~1
protected by a hard invariant. The calibrated sequence uses J3 gravity
feed-forward and a disclosed Joint~2 positioning offset around engagement.
In all six grasp-and-return trials, the operator manually positions the
object between the open jaws at the calibrated grasp-ready pose before
governed closure, lift and return. The placement protocol for the three
repetitions without video is author-confirmed; their logs record robot
execution. Console logs, passive CAN captures, source/configuration
snapshots, video mappings and checksums support the intervention experiment,
three video-backed trials and three logged repetitions. There is no
independent force/torque measurement or autonomous perception-to-grasp
evaluation (\suppsection{S12}).

\subsection{Runtime measurement}
\label{sec:timing-methods}
The latency benchmark times the complete $(o,a)\mapsto(a',\mathrm{info})$
call, including allocations, per-call constraint construction and
diagnostics. It uses 2,048 saved inputs per task, five fresh processes and
256 warm-up calls per implementation/process. Identical per-task input
sequences are shared across arms, with task/arm order randomized in 64-input
blocks. Durations are untrimmed integer nanoseconds; cyclic garbage
collection is disabled, and timer overhead is recorded without subtraction.
Each process uses CPU~0 and one native numerical thread. OSQP reuses its
problem and warm start; one-time construction is reported separately.
Policy/VLM inference, input generation, simulation and robot communication
are outside the interval. \suppsection{S8} records host/software versions,
tolerances, raw arrays, source hashes and the complete workload design.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}
\label{sec:statistics}
The training seed, not the evaluation episode, is the unit for policy
comparisons. We report ten-seed means and all plotted seed values. Selected
contrasts use paired differences by seed identifier and exact percentile
bootstrap distributions over those ten differences, computed by convolution.
Intervals are descriptive, not multiplicity-adjusted tests. Same-policy
Stage~1 contrasts are distinguished from separately trained Stage~2 and
cross-regime pairs. The preventive BATTERY and PCB cross-regime contrasts
also have recorded episode-seed mismatches, so pairing is only at
training-seed level (\suppref{tab:submitted-11}). The shifted reset study
uses the same estimator and training-seed pairing. Its changes from
historical in-range estimates include episode-sampling variation because the
schedules differ; within/outside-support strata are descriptive
(\suppsection{S14}). Procedural instances are the units in the geometry
study. VLM counts are descriptive over paired images and commands; views
clustered within 28 configurations are not independent physical replicates,
and no inferential cross-model test is reported.

\subsection{Records and provenance}
\label{sec:provenance}
Every experimental record is linked to the applicable environment, policy,
constraint, margin and outcome specifications. The curated source map links
all main and supplementary figures and tables to data or executable source.
Correction notes retain affected historical files. Independent physical
calibration and unresolved backend identities remain provenance limits.
Figure~\ref{fig:seed-distributions} was regenerated from the retained
seed-level data using a supplied replacement plotting script; the original
plotting script remains unavailable.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Training with the governor reverses a post-hoc failure}
\label{sec:policy-results}
Circuit-board extraction shows most clearly that a governor and its policy act
as one system. Applied after SAC training, the componentwise
\texttt{box\_clip} restriction yields 0.0\% safe completion; with the same
restriction active during training, safe completion is 100.0\%
(Table~\ref{tab:policy-summary}). All ten training seeds show the reversal
(Figure~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}a). The frozen policy itself is
competent: under TRiX it reaches 100.0\%, so the post-hoc failure lies in the
restriction rather than the policy. A policy trained under the box learns to
work inside the authority the box retains. The restriction is identical in
both settings; only the regime changes.

\subsection{Matched controls separate computation from specification}
\label{sec:matched-results}
Screw removal holds the constraint fixed and varies only how the correction
is computed. Under frozen-policy evaluation, \texttt{oracle\_tangent},
\texttt{qp\_matched} and TRiX each reach 88.7\% safe completion for SAC and
60.0\% for PPO, against 0.0\% without a governor, and the matched constrained
controls reach 100\% for both algorithms with filter-aware training. Given
the same admissible set, the methods agree exactly. The comparisons that
follow therefore vary what each governor represents and knows rather than how
it computes; computational cost is compared at the end of the Results.

\subsection{Missing task context limits what training can recover}
\label{sec:context-results}
Training does not compensate for everything a restriction leaves out. On
crank removal, the static restriction stays at 0.0\% after filter-aware SAC
training, while the task-conditioned governor reaches 49.4\%, with seeds
clustered near 0\% or 100\% (Figure~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}c). The two
governors differ in both angle-conditioned axial bounds and task-frame
transverse representation, so this pair measures their combined effect.

Snap-fit release separates even more sharply. Within the training reset
ranges, safe completion is 5.2\% under the static restriction and 96.5\%
under TRiX (Figure~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}b). These are separately
trained policy-and-governor systems that also differ in latch-phase
conditioning and margin, so the gap measures the systems rather than the
governor's causal contribution alone. Under the shifted resets reported
below, these rates fall to 3.5\% and 59.9\%, respectively.

\subsection{Simpler restrictions suffice when nothing is missing}
\label{sec:sufficient-results}
Where a simple restriction already carries the information a task needs,
adaptation makes it sufficient. On prying, filter-aware SAC reaches 88.5\%
under the static restriction and 89.1\% under the task-conditioned one, with
closely aligned seed distributions and the same zero-performing seed
(Figure~\ref{fig:seed-distributions}d). On circuit-board extraction in this
regime, the box reaches 100.0\% and TRiX 99.8\%. These two arms also differ
in torque margin, a nominal 0.100~N\,m per-axis bound against a robust coupled
radius of approximately 0.087087~N\,m, so the pair measures geometry and
margin together rather than box-versus-disk geometry alone.

Battery peeling isolates the specification. Earlier arms compared the 20-N
flagged peel boundary with a 15-N preventive deformation threshold. When
componentwise and TRiX arms share the same preventive target and margin
construction, both reach 100\% safe completion. An exact-arm audit corrected
a historical aggregation that had pooled similarly named BATTERY arms, setting
Stage~1 SAC/TRiX safe completion to 20.0\% with original records retained
(\suppsection{S9.4}). \suppsection{S9} reports the full policy tables and
\suppref{tab:submitted-11} the training-seed intervals and pairing exceptions.
On some tasks the nominal policy itself fails, leaving every frozen-policy arm
at zero, which limits the ranking those cells can support.

\subsection{Learned constraint models lose required authority}
\label{sec:representation-results}
Constraint representation matters independently of the policy. On 2,000
held-out circuit-board proposals, componentwise clipping produces 2.9\%
feasible commands, a learned action-affine model 0.0\%, a learned nonlinear
model 78.0\% after one correction and 92.3\% after iteration, and the
analytical oracle and exact projector 100\%. The affine result stays at
0.0\% as fitting samples grow from 500 to 20,000, placing its failure in the
representation rather than the data. This single-seed study compares the
implemented representations, not learning in general or complete safety
algorithms.

A procedural study extends the comparison to 200 admissible sets drawn from
box, ellipsoid, polytope, state-scaled, rotating and phase-gated families,
each with a required action inside its exact set. The context-free box
excludes that action in 124 instances and retains it in 76. Because this
partition is defined by the box's own exclusion, it is a constructed
calibration rather than an independent test of the box; the informative
results are the other arms (\suppref{tab:procedural-geometry}). Exact
projection retains the required action in 100\% of both groups, and the
context-conditioned oracle in 36.3\% and 100\%, respectively. The learned
affine model retains it in 0\% of both groups, including the group where a box
succeeds. Every arm returns feasible commands on 400 sampled proposals per
instance, partly because approximate arms share the inscribed-box bounds, so
feasibility and retained authority are distinct properties. Models are fitted
separately on each evaluated geometry, so the study measures representation
capacity rather than transfer of one learned model (\suppsection{S10}).

\subsection{Per-step admissibility does not secure outcomes outside the training range}
\label{sec:ood-results}
The prospectively declared shifted-reset evaluation shows where per-step
governance ends. In the primary outside-support shell, snap-fit safe
completion is 59.9\% under TRiX and 3.5\% under static, down from the in-range
96.5\% and 5.2\%; as in range, this compares complete policy-and-governor
systems and does not isolate the governor's causal contribution. Latch
fractures, including timeouts, occur in 401 and 468 of 1,000 episodes per arm.
Destructive completions, which combine completed episodes with fracture or
accumulated damage and exclude timeouts, number 369 and 886
(\suppref{tab:snap-ood-attribution}). The advantage of TRiX in safe completion
is therefore far larger than its reduction in fractures.

A post-hoc replay audit locates the mechanism. Every TRiX command lies inside
the frozen executable bounds, yet all 401 TRiX fractures begin through
overdeflection in the softer-latch stratum, before any pull damage, while all
480 TRiX episodes in the harder-latch stratum complete safely. Admissible
commands, applied one step at a time, drove a softer latch into a damaging
state. These counts are observations within the sampled stiffness intervals,
not a safety bound over them; \suppsection{S14.4} reports the full partition,
the traces and how the executed margins relate to a later, tighter registry.

The other tasks shift less. Crank removal reaches 47.6\% under TRiX and
0.0\% under the static arm, with 22 destructive completions per 1,000
episodes under TRiX. Matched screw arms remain equal at 87.9\% for SAC and
59.9\% for PPO. Circuit-board extraction gives 0.0\%/99.6\% in Stage~1 and
100.0\%/99.8\% in Stage~2 for box/TRiX, and preventive battery arms give
100.0\%/100.0\%. Prying has a deterministic physical reset and is outside this
evaluation. \suppsection{S14}, \suppref{tab:ood-safe} and
\suppref{tab:ood-outcomes} report all three declared conditions, changes,
intervals and outcome categories; the original in-range results are
unchanged.

\subsection{Governance intercepts violations in foundation-model commands}
\label{sec:vlm-results}
Three recorded VLM runs received the same 91 circuit-board images and prompt,
giving 273 decisions. The labels Claude Sonnet, GPT-4o and Gemini identify the
configured aliases and recorded runs; resolved backend versions are not
recorded. The decisions partition into 16 refusals, 219 admissible commands
and 38 inadmissible commands, with no ungroundable output
(Table~\ref{tab:vlm-results}), and each of the 257 grounded commands was
replayed with and without governance.

TRiX prevents all 31 observed constraint-violation cases and introduces none.
A case counts as prevented only when the unfiltered replay violates a
constraint and the governed replay from the same restored state and seed does
not; changing a command earns no credit. GPT-4o contributes nine prevented
cases and Gemini 22, while the three inadmissible Claude commands caused no
unfiltered violation. In one example, GPT-4o's response to the
\texttt{s06\_occluded} image requests \texttt{LIFT} at magnitude 0.8,
grounding to $(0,0,0.8)$: the unfiltered replay violates the constraint on
40/40 steps and the governed replay on 0/40. Every replay runs the full
40-step horizon without task completion or mechanical failure, so the
measured outcome is prevented constraint violation, not fractures or
successful extraction. The commands were elicited from visibility-scaled
images whose displayed tilts can exceed the model's intact-board range, and
the result measures governance of those recorded commands.

The failures differ by model. Thirty-five images elicit an inadmissible
proposal in at least one run and none in all three. Claude's three
inadmissible cases and GPT-4o's nine share no image, even when grouped by base
configuration, so no Gemini failure set could produce a three-way overlap. The
study measures one proposal per image across repeated views of 28
configurations; it evaluates governance of proposals rather than an
autonomous agent loop or hallucination detection.

\subsection{Governance changes what a physical robot executes}
\label{sec:hardware-results}
On the B601-RS arm, TRiX sits in the command path and its decisions change
what the motors execute. It rejects an invalid symbolic transition and a
proposed $+50^\circ$ motion of protected Joint~1, producing no physical
target. It projects an excessive $+50^\circ$ Joint~2 proposal to $+18^\circ$,
and only the corrected target is transmitted and executed. The allowed return
finishes with Joint~1 drift of $+0.007^\circ$.

The frozen final implementation then completes three video-backed
grasp-and-return trials with the object retained
(Figure~\ref{fig:b601-hardware}), each with ten \texttt{ALLOW} decisions, for
30 governed decisions in total. The largest absolute Joint~1 return drift is
$0.027^\circ$ and the largest start-to-final residual over Joints~1--6 is
$0.122^\circ$. Three further logged repetitions complete normally and are
counted separately from the video-backed trials. In all six, the operator
places the object between the open jaws at a calibrated grasp pose, and the
governed sequence closes the gripper, lifts and returns. The arm carries no
independent wrench sensor: these trials establish the command boundary on
physical hardware, and force-level calibration of the six task models is the
next step (\suppsection{S12}).

\subsection{Runtime cost depends on the implementation}
\label{sec:runtime-benchmark}
TRiX adds microseconds to each command. A benchmark of 225,280
complete-filter calls across 22 existing implementations, six tasks and five
fresh processes, on the recorded Intel Core Ultra 9 275HX host, gives matched
screw median/95th-percentile latencies of 1.255/1.556~$\mu$s for production
scalar TRiX, 12.578/15.534~$\mu$s for active-set QP and 14.924/19.895~$\mu$s
for reused OSQP. All matched arms pass the declared numerical agreement checks;
the maximum OSQP action-coordinate difference is $1.013\times10^{-6}$. Across
the six registered TRiX arms, medians range from 1.123 to 11.770~$\mu$s.
Implementation matters as much as formulation: the NumPy analytical
implementation takes 18.163~$\mu$s by median, slower than both QP medians for
the same geometry. \suppref{tab:runtime-latency} reports every implementation
and its process variation. All outliers are retained, and these are measured
costs on the recorded host rather than worst-case or end-to-end robot latency.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Execution governance is a property of a system, not of a filter alone.
Circuit-board extraction shows one restriction failing as a post-hoc filter
and succeeding as a training-time constraint, and crank removal shows that
training cannot supply context a restriction omits. Evaluations of runtime
governors should therefore report post-hoc governance and filter-aware
learning separately, because each supports a different claim.

Specification and representation, rather than solver, decide the outcomes.
Matching the command set makes analytical and QP controls coincide on screw
removal, and matching the preventive specification removes the battery
difference. The procedural study separates feasibility from retained
authority, and the learned nonlinear circuit-board correction shows how much
an expressive learned model, iterated, can recover. The contribution is an
auditable execution contract together with an experimental map of when each
kind of restriction suffices, rather than a universal ranking of symbolic
projection over learned or classical methods. The timing advantage likewise
belongs to the scalar implementation: the NumPy analytical version is slower
than both matched QP controls.

The results also mark where per-step governance ends, which defines what must
be added to it. Outside training support, every TRiX command on snap-fit
release stayed within bounds while fractures fell only from 468 to 401 per
1,000 episodes: admissible commands do not by themselves secure the state they
produce. In battery peeling, an internal short can keep heating after force is
removed, beyond the reach of any current-command filter. In snap-fit release,
suppressing a premature pull does not supply the latch deflection the task
needs. A correct projector also enforces whatever boundary it is given: the
20-N flagged battery limit allows deformation that begins at 15~N. And
probabilistic tightening can remove all authority: for the crank axial family,
$L/\sigma=5/1.235\simeq4.05$, whereas the declared multiplier is approximately
4.488, a specification-feasibility failure detailed in \suppsection{S13}.
Each limit points to a concrete extension: state-aware constraints,
sequence-level planning and calibrated specifications.

These boundaries are empirical, and the evidence reports them in full. The
prescribed task-specific ordering holds in 96 of 105 perturbation cases; the
nine exceptions comprise six PCB boundary/model cases, one CRANK feasibility
failure and two BATTERY completion trade-offs with zero destructive
completions in both arms (\suppsection{S7}). A prospective BAYONET test is
inconclusive because neither policy algorithm reached its predeclared
competence gate, and it is reported as such.

Across its three foundations, the study carries one execution contract from
learned policies to foundation-model proposals to physical motors. The model
study shows governance intercepting real generative commands, on
visibility-scaled images of one task. The hardware study shows rejection and
projection changing what physical actuators execute and supporting repeated
manipulation, with a calibrated grasp, manual object placement and a separate
joint-space contract. Neither calibrates the benchmark's force, torque or
damage thresholds; that requires independent physical measurements across
contact uncertainty, object variation and model mismatch. The next step is to
join the three layers: calibrated force and damage models, state estimation
and sequence planning, measured on hardware against the same contract.
Explicit execution governance is most informative when its proposal
distribution, task context and physical specification are evaluated together,
and TRiX makes each of them visible.
